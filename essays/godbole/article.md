---
title: | 
    Personal Identity and Mind Uploading: A Cartesian Analysis
---

Advances in computational neuroscience and large-scale brain mapping have opened the possibility for a new form of immortality integrating both technology and consciousness, a process known as mind uploading. This idea proposes that if the structural and functional organization of the brain can be sufficiently mapped and simulated, the resulting computational model could reproduce the cognitive processes associated with memory, personality, and conscious experience [@sandberg2008whole]. Yet, these assumptions are contingent on the idea that consciousness is independent of the biological body, allowing for the mind to be "uploaded" into a medium aside from the body. Mind uploading raises a central scientific question: if consciousness can be replicated and transferred to another medium, is the resulting uploaded individual's personal identity preserved? Using René Descartes's dualist framework, this essay argues that mind uploading fails to preserve personal identity, as it reproduces neural activity without capturing the non-physical mind that defines true consciousness.

Contemporary neuroscience increasingly investigates personal identity in terms of large-scale neural organization and patterns of activity across distributed brain networks. Studies of the neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) attempt to identify the minimal neural mechanism required to produce a conscious perception or experience [@koch2016neural]. If conscious states correspond to particular neural configurations, then mind uploading is, in principle, a viable technology that can reproduce consciousness in an uploaded mainframe. Long before modern neuroscience attempted to understand consciousness within neural systems, Descartes addressed this question through his method of doubt, defining one of his absolute truths as "I saw that while I could pretend that I had no body and that there was no world and no place for me to be in, I still couldn't pretend that I didn't exist" [@descartes2017discourse]. Descartes' reasoning suggests that conscious awareness possesses a form of independence from the body's physical properties, a claim that contrasts with modern neuroscience's attempts to reduce cognition to neural processes.

Modern cognitive neuroscience often describes the brain as a highly integrated network composed of specialized subsystems responsible for perception, memory, and decision making [@sporns2011networks]. In a similar conceptual structure, Descartes viewed the relationship between mind and body as analogous to a conductor directing an orchestra. The conductor can instruct specific instruments to play certain songs, imagine melodies, and develop songs; however, they are unable to produce audible music without the orchestra. This separation provides a philosophical space for mind uploading: if the mind is distinct from the body, it may be transferrable to a new medium.

However, a challenge to this view comes from the neuroscientific evidence linking consciousness to biology. Contemporary neuroscience strongly correlates conscious experience with coordinated activity across large-scale neural networks, particularly within thalamocortical systems associated with awareness and attention [@dehaene2011experimental], suggesting that conscious experience depends on coordinated neural activity and therefore ceases when brain function stops. Yet, through a Cartesian lens, correlation does not establish metaphysical identity. As @cottingham1985cartesian observed, Descartes does not deny that the brain plays a role in thought; rather, he denies that thought is reducible to matter. Following the conductor analogy, the brain may serve as the instrument through which the mind operates, just as the orchestra gives audible form to the conductor's motions. If the orchestra is disbanded, the music stops, but that does not logically entail that the composer ceases to exist. All it suggests is that there is no longer a medium through which the composition is externally expressed. In the same way, biology describes the conditions under which consciousness is expressed, not the essence of consciousness itself [@cottingham1985cartesian].

Although Descartes distinguishes the mind from the body, he also emphasizes the necessity of their union. The body is not irrelevant; it is the medium through which the soul experiences emotion, sensation, and outward expression [@descartes2017discourse]. Even if the soul is immortal and capable of existing independently, immortality alone does not clarify whether it can operate in isolation. If the soul survives bodily death, the consequent question is whether it requires a new medium through which to act and express itself. In this way, Descartes suggests that while the mind may not be reducible to the body, it nevertheless depends upon some form of embodiment for the full realization of human experience.

Returning to the conductor analogy, what would happen if the conductor were to upload their compositions to a digital medium? The resulting performance would differ fundamentally from one produced by a live orchestra. The nuance and expressiveness that define the original would be constrained by the capabilities of the digital format. The same critique applies to mind uploading. The personal identity of an individual may be compromised if the digital medium does not allow for the correct external ways of expression. If the conductor only had access to three instruments their creative freedom would be significantly reduced compared to a full orchestra. Descartes anticipates the same problem when comparing human beings with robots. More specifically, Descartes adds that "[e]ven though such machines might do some things as well as we do them, or perhaps even better, they would be bound to fail in others" [@descartes2017discourse]. He reasons that although a robot might be physically capable of many things, it could fail others, just as mind uploading fails to encapsulate the expression of the human brain. He elaborates that "[r]eason is a universal instrument that can be used in all kinds of situations" whereas a machine's organs require "some particular disposition for each particular action," making it "impossible for a machine to have enough different organs to make it act in all the contingencies of life" [@descartes1911meditations]. Just like the digital world, there would not be enough tools to allow the human consciousness to do anything. It is obstructed by the fixation on the specific technology.

Mind uploading digitally uploads the mind through scanning and dissecting the brain, recording every neuron fired, synapse, and neural network [@chalmers2014mind]. From Descartes' perspective, this approach misunderstands what the soul fundamentally is. If the mind has no parts, then a scan that captures "parts" of mental functioning is only capturing the biological pathway through which the mind operates, not the thinking substance itself. In this way, Descartes provides not just a historical philosophical background but a direct critique of the metaphysical assumptions behind mind uploading: if the self is fundamentally indivisible, then any technology designed to hold the soul can only replicate its processes, not the substance that constitutes personal identity. Descartes's dualism provides a crucial problem within mind uploading and the fact of personal identity existing within an individual once uploaded.

Ultimately, the debate over mind uploading is not just about future technology but about the nature of the self. Descartes' dualism challenges the optimism surrounding digital immortality by arguing that the true self cannot be captured by any system. If consciousness is unified and inseparable from the embodied experiences through which it expresses itself, then a digital copy could never preserve personal identity. Mind uploading may one day grant immortality, but from a Cartesian perspective, it cannot guarantee the survival of personal identity. Whether or not Descartes' dualism is ultimately correct, his framework forces future research in artificial consciousness to confront a fundamental question: is identity defined by the biological architecture of the brain, or by the persistence of an invisible substance of consciousness?
