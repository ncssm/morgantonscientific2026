---
title: Effects of Caffeine Concentration on Survivability, Locomotor Activity, and Memory in Drosophila melanogaster
short_title: "Effects of Caffeine Concentration on Drosophila melanogaster"
abstract: |
    Caffeine is one of the most widely consumed substances worldwide and is known to affect neural and behavioural processes based on concentration. Despite humans consuming caffeine on a daily basis, the effects of chronic exposure are not fully understood in terms of locomotion, survival and memory. In this study, *Drosophila melanogaster* is used as the model organism to investigate the impact of various concentrations of caffeine on survivability, short-term memory and locomotion (negative geotaxis). All flies were wild type and were assigned to 5 different treatment groups: no sugar and no caffeine (control), 4% sugar and no caffeine (control), 4% sugar with 0.01% caffeine, 4% sugar with 0.025% caffeine, and 4% sugar with 0.05% caffeine. Survival was monitored daily, while locomotor performance was assessed through repeated climbing assays, and memory was evaluated through a memory assay with the use of a y-shaped maze. Overall, our findings, even though not statistically significant, suggested that moderate caffeine consumption (0.025% caffeine) doesn't impact survival, while higher concentrations of caffeine may potentially have negative effects on locomotor function and memory. This study highlights the importance of caffeine exposure and different concentrations using *Drosophila melanogaster* as a model for examining effects in relevance to more complex organisms.

## Introduction

Caffeine is a common stimulant consumed daily by many Americans. Roughly 69% consume at least one beverage with caffeine every day, on average consuming 210 mg each day. Caffeine is commonly consumed through coffee, carbonated soft drinks, tea, and energy drinks, respectively [@mitchell2025beverage]. Caffeine can affect the nervous and metabolic system of many organisms, impacting memory, movement, and survival with long-term exposure typically having concentration-dependent effects.

Moderate caffeine exposure raises arousal levels, allowing feelings of alertness and focus, while a dose too high may cause anxiety, impairing the previously mentioned factors. Higher concentrations of caffeine have been shown to decrease life span and locomotive abilities in the green bottle fly [@naing2025caffeine]. Caffeine in extreme concentrations can be harmful and potentially lethal depending on the person ingesting it and their cardiovascular health. Caffeine is especially risky because it can lead to abuse and dependence when not used in moderation. People tend to become addicted to caffeine because it can increase cognitive performance and provide energy by chemically activating different neuronal pathways. However, it is difficult to ingest a lethal dose of one caffeine product alone, such as coffee, tea, etc.; it is relatively rare that humans ingest a lethal amount [@cappelletti2015caffeine].

Sweeteners are substances that stimulate sweet taste receptors and are found all around the world in many different types of foods due to their ability to enhance flavor. They play a huge role in modern diets as they affect public health outcomes such as diabetes, obesity and metabolic diseases [@rippe2013sucrose]. Potential effects of excessive sugar intake are being studied and are raising concerns among the health community. Sucrose is a disaccharide and nutritive sweetener [@pubchem2026sucrose]. It is composed of 50% glucose and 50% fructose [@groves2022sucrose]. It is one of the most widely consumed nutritive sweeteners worldwide as it is also commonly called table sugar [@angelin2024sweeteners]. Sucrose is commonly used in biological research to ensure consistent food intake, especially in experiments involving *Drosophila melanogaster* to examine other compounds [@wang2016sucralose]. It can encourage ingestion without serving as the primary experimental sweetener.

*Drosophila melanogaster* (commonly known as fruit flies) is a widely used model organism in biological research due to its many genetic, physiological, and practical advantages. For instance, they take up little to no space allowing scientists to conduct many studies at once by simply placing them in multiple vials. They are also relatively cheap and efficient, especially compared to many other model organisms such as mice, monkeys, etc. These conditions make it easier to have large sample sizes and repeat experiments. Due to their short lifespan and ability to quickly reproduce, traits can be studied across multiple generations to identify specific effects that happen. Additionally, their short lifespan allows scientists to study the effects of a specific stimulus over their lifetime. Because they display characteristics based on age, it is easier to study their effects [@tolwinski2017drosophila]. Fruit flies share a significant amount of genes with humans; approximately 60–75% involve cell division, organ formation, and disease pathways (Alzheimer's, diabetes, and cancer) [@spaude2025drosophila]. Having 4 pairs of chromosomes makes fruit flies genetically simple to study, which allows researchers to easily identify genetic patterns and connect them to more complex organisms [@hartmann2017crossovers]. Flies are a common model, and they are crucial for research due to their many benefits pertaining to scientific studies.

A memory assay was chosen to test if different concentrations of caffeine will have a positive or negative impact on memory. In the memory assay, one side had a drop of tea tree oil, which tends to be a repellent for fruit flies as they find the smell overwhelming [@smith2024teatree]. Additionally, a survivability assay was chosen to demonstrate a caffeinated diet and the effects it has on the overall well-being of fruit flies. A climbing assay was chosen to see if different concentrations of caffeine caused the flies to be distracted or more focused while climbing up, which relates to negative geotaxis. Under controlled conditions using fruit flies, we can understand caffeine's effects at different concentrations allowing for a better understanding of how intake levels can impact behavior, cognition, and overall health.

Since caffeine and sugar are in many diets all around the world, observing the effects of these substances, specifically caffeine at different concentrations will allow us to see how the consumption by the fruit flies can reflect the effects on more complex organisms, like humans. We hypothesize that higher concentration of caffeine negatively impacts the locomotive ability, memory, and survival rate of fruit flies while a low concentration may not have much of an effect on those factors. A caffeine diet in moderation will have a positive effect on the factors mentioned above [@mclellan2016caffeine]. However, the effects of different caffeine concentrations on behavior and survival are not completely understood.

## Materials and Methods

### Chemicals and Reagents

Sucrose, active dry yeast (Fleischmann's), potato flakes (Carolina Biological Supply Company), blue food coloring (Walmart), tea tree oil (Nature's Truth), and caffeine tablets (Walgreens) were used in this study. The sucrose (Ward's Science) used was standard table sugar and had no added ingredients. The caffeine tablets used were 200 mg and were pure caffeine.

### Model Organisms

40 young wild type *Drosophila melanogaster* purchased from PetSmart were used in this experiment. 8 flies were randomly assigned to 5 different vials representing different experimental groups. Sex of flies was not controlled for and may have contributed to variability in results. All the experimental flies were placed in labeled plastic vials at room temperature.

### Experimental Design

5 dietary conditions were tested. The first group was no sugar and no caffeine (control), 4% sugar and no caffeine (control), 4% sugar and 0.01% caffeine, 4% sugar and 0.025% caffeine, and 4% sugar and 0.05% caffeine. Each solution was made by heating water and diluting caffeine pills and sugar to achieve the ideal solution.

### Preparation of Sugar and Caffeine Solutions

The study was conducted by dividing the flies into the groups mentioned above. The diet consisted of one tablespoon of potato flakes which were hydrated with the different diet supplements mentioned above. For instance, the 4% sugar and 0.01% caffeine solution was used to hydrate the potato flakes in the vial studying the effect of low caffeine. The stock caffeine solution was made by dissolving a 200 mg caffeine tablet in 100 mL of heated water. A series of dilutions were performed to obtain the 0.02%, 0.05%, and 0.10% solutions. These solutions were prepared at double the desired concentration so they could later be diluted to reach the wanted concentrations. A sucrose stock solution of 8% was made by dissolving 4 g of sucrose in 50 mL of distilled water. Appropriate volumes of the sucrose and caffeinated solutions were mixed to create the desired caffeine concentrations of 0.010%, 0.025%, and 0.05%. For the 4% sucrose solution with no caffeine, 4 g of sucrose was dissolved in 100 mL of water.

### Yeast Paste

The making of the yeast paste stayed constant and each vial with yeast paste was made with their respective solutions (1.5 mL) and mixed with yeast (0.5 g) and blue dye (1.5 µL), with an additional 2 mL of the corresponding liquid being added on top. The yeast paste was evenly spread and allowed to absorb into the potato flakes of each vial for approximately 7 minutes before the flies were placed inside the vial on top of the potato flakes.

### Fly Survival Rate Assessment

Fly survivability was assessed every day. At the start of each day, the number of living flies in each vial was counted.

### Locomotive Performance by Negative Geotaxis

Flies from one vial were transferred into an empty vial and a second empty vial was placed upside down on top with a line measuring 14 cm. The flies were tapped down 3 times to help initiate the trial where we recorded the number of flies that crossed the 14 cm mark in 30 seconds, taking note of the percentage of successful flies. The flies rested for 30 seconds in between trials, and we conducted 9 trials per experimental group. After the testing occurred, the flies were returned to their original food vial, and the procedure was repeated for all experimental groups.

### Memory Retention Assay

The memory assay was conducted using a Y-shaped maze that was 3D printed. The maze had a small hole on the bottom that allowed us to tap the flies into it. During the training phase, a drop of tea tree oil was placed onto a cotton ball as a repellent while the opposite side contained a sugar solution as a reward. The flies were allowed to choose between arms and at the end of each 5-minute trial, the number of flies on each side was recorded. The training was conducted 4 times per group and after a 4-hour rest period, the flies were tested in the same maze with no reward or repellent.

### Data Analysis

The results of the experiment were expressed in graphs using SEM (standard error of the mean). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the results due to the multiple variables present. Each assay is provided with its own graph and is spread across a number of days. The level of significance was demonstrated at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses as well as graphs were made using GraphPad PRISM software.

## Discussion

Flies typically don't consume caffeine and even a small amount of caffeine is actually proven to be detrimental to their survival [@naing2025caffeine]. Although the flies were provided with a very low percentage of caffeine that was diluted as well as a sugar solution to make it more appealing, it is not completely certain whether the food was consumed by the flies. Variability in feeding behaviour and other factors may have led to the flies not eating the food at all. This could have been another reason for their decreased locomotor activity, survival, and memory activity. Although we attempted to conduct a smurf assay by putting blue dye in their food to determine if the flies were eating the food, we believe we may not have added enough dye due to not seeing a substantial change in color near their gut section and thus did not include that data.

The flies we originally tested for our experiment all came out of the same stock and 2 different vials. This led us to estimate age based on size, but that is not an accurate way to determine the age of the fruit flies. Different ages of fruit flies may have influenced the survivability as well as other behavioural outcomes such as the memory assay and the climbing assay. The age of the fruit flies not being completely known could introduce additional variability into our results.

Due to inconsistencies in procedural execution, we had flies that we lost over the course of 6 days. This typically happened during fly transferring. For instance, when transferring flies into empty vials for the climbing assay, some flies escaped or sustained injuries. Additionally, during the memory assays, due to the entrance leading into the y-shaped maze being too small, the likelihood of the flies being accidentally crushed had increased. During both the memory and climbing assays, the flies were tapped multiple times which could have induced physical stress, potentially affecting locomotor performance, memory assay outcomes, and survival. Variability in the force that was applied among different trials could be another source of experimental error.

Losses due to human error introduce uncertainty in truly knowing if those flies would have survived under their respective dietary treatments. Additionally, due to the deaths, it caused the sample sizes per treatment group to be decreased over our trial period, potentially leading to inconsistencies in data. In general, having a smaller sample size leads to less applicable and accurate conclusions. The relatively small sample size to begin with also could have interfered with the accuracy of our conclusions.

Conducting the memory assay the same day as the climbing assay may have impacted the results of the climbing assay, tiring the flies out through the training amount. It is believed that the memory assay hindered their ability to efficiently climb as they may have been tired out due to the excessive tapping needed to transfer the fruit flies in and out of the y-shaped maze. Even in the following day, the 0.025% caffeine group's data could have been affected by the memory assay, as flies may have gotten injured or severely weakened due to excessive tapping to get the flies out of the maze. Due to the flies not climbing, we were unable to observe if they had remembered their training.

Caffeine ultimately had a detrimental effect on the flies' climbing ability, as seen in @Figure_2 by comparing the results of the experimental groups with the controls. This could potentially be caused by the caffeine leading to sleep deprivation for the flies, which has been proven to decrease climbing ability for wild-type fruit flies [@ito2017sleep]. The caffeine could have lessened the amount and quality of the flies' sleep, which could have ended up causing their climbing ability to decrease, especially over a prolonged period of time.

In addition, high sucrose diets have a negative impact on *Drosophila melanogaster*'s climbing ability and locomotion, which is a potential reason why the first control group consistently had the most successful results, and the longer the flies were on the diet the further the results got from the no sugar, no caffeine group (@Figure_3) [@bazzell2012dietary].

Because the memory assay was only run one day, we are unsure if the results can be used for future generalizations. Many more trials would need to occur for conclusive results. In addition, not all the flies went to the reward side in training when given time to explore, so that would not encourage them to go to that same side later from their memory when the reward was taken away. By training 3, the flies should have been more consistently going to the reward side, but they did not, as seen in @Figure_5.

## Conclusion

This study investigated the different effects that various amounts of caffeine had on survival, locomotor activity, and memory in *Drosophila melanogaster*. Overall, we observed that caffeine exposure didn't produce any type of statistically significant effect on survivability. However, locomotor performance, assessed by a climbing assay, was shown to decrease over increasing caffeine concentrations. The 0.05% caffeine treatment group was shown to have the most negative impact. Additionally, though not statistically significant, memory performance showed a downward trend associated with caffeine concentration. These results suggest caffeine may interfere with cognitive processes, though further research is needed. We also had several limitations and unwanted variables such as uncertainty in fly age, variability in diet consumption, and unwanted deaths in transfer. Despite these experimental errors, this study allows for evidence supporting the idea that caffeine's effects on *Drosophila melanogaster* are concentration dependent. The results given from the experiment may contribute to a broader understanding of how chronic caffeine consumption may affect neural and motor functions on not just *Drosophila melanogaster*, but even in humans.
