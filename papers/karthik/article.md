short_title: "Engineering a Low-Cost Environment for Magnetic Hyperthermia"
abstract:
    Magnetic Hyperthermia (MH) is a targeted cancer therapy utilizing magnetic nanoparticles and alternating magnetic fields (AMFs) to induce heat via Néel and Brownian Relaxation. Despite the potential that MH has a noninvasive treatment for cancer, the cost of equipment necessary for research is expensive and relatively untested. This project investigates a low-cost method of experimentation involving synthesis and setup procedures, along with the utilization of DIY instrumentation.
    Magnetite nanoparticles were successfully synthesized via coprecipitation with Sodium Hydroxide. The optical properties were characterized with a UV-Vis spectrum that confirmed that the particles had similar properties in general. A low-cost Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) instrument was also designed to compare the size of different samples of the nanoparticles. Thermochromic phantom tissue was designed to test the effects of MH, but the initial setup involving a coil was unsuccessful, as the magnetic field generated by the coil was weak due to the insufficient current supplied by the function generator. Consequently, the project pivoted to using a motorized rotating magnetic field. This was successful in producing a temperature increase of up to 1.5℃ in 15 minutes, although the frequency of the AMF was not fast enough for the clinical procedure.
    The project was successful in developing low-cost methods of experimenting with MH which can be used to further research factors that affect Specific Absorption Rates along with other methods that can be utilized to optimize the procedure.

## Introduction
Magnetic Hyperthermia is a method used to combat cancer that was first developed in 1957. This method utilizes magnetic nanoparticles and low-frequency AC current. The AC current gets the nanoparticles spinning while switching directions when the direction of the current flow changes, generating heat through molecular friction and relaxation, effectively destroying the surrounding tissue without affecting other tissue (@Figure_1). When functionalized with biocompatible coatings, the biofilm layer prevents harmful interactions between the particles and the body, while also allowing the nanoparticles to be used to perform targeted drug delivery, enabling it to enhance procedures like chemotherapy [@liu_comprehensive_2020]. Currently, the main use for magnetic hyperthermia is cancer therapy, but there are also potential applications for the inactivation of viruses by inducing heat, in addition to the usage of magnetic hyperthermia for bacterial infections, especially where antibiotic-resistant bacteria are present.

There is much potential for magnetic hyperthermia to become a widely used procedure, but there is more experimentation that must be done to optimize the procedure and minimize the safety concerns. This is due to the fact that there are numerous variables that affect the procedure, which include nanoparticle size, particle shape, particle coating, and alternating magnetic field frequency, all of which must be considered while ensuring that safety standards are followed. Performing experiments on the procedure can be very expensive, even if just doing in vitro experiments. Getting access to the necessary chemicals and the specific structure of magnetic nanoparticles, in addition to other sensing technology, can be very expensive, making it difficult to experiment with the many variables affecting the procedure. The alternating magnetic field and power source setup can also add to the expenses necessary to study the procedure. If these expenses can be decreased, it will enable more people to conduct experiments and contribute to the existing research, which can aid in the improvement of the procedure with the eventual possibility of the procedure being to save lives in hospitals worldwide.

### Research Goals and Criteria
This project focuses on addressing the issue that experimentation on magnetic hyperthermia is very expensive, but is necessary to further optimize the procedure for efficiency and safety so it can be effectively used across the world as a treatment method for cancer along with possibly other diseases. In order to achieve that goal it is necessary to develop a repeatable procedure for iron oxide magnetite ($Fe_3$$O_4$) synthesis, approximate the size of the nanoparticles, and to simulate the procedure. These synthesis, characterization, and testing procedures should allow for the easy variation of variables like particle size and alternating magnetic field frequency while properly mimicking the application of magnetic hyperthermia on human flesh, all for a budget of less than $200.
## Methodology
### Iron Oxide Magnetite Magnetic Nanoparticle Synthesis
The coprecipitation method was used to synthesize the nanoparticles. The initial procedure was modeled after a co-precipitation protocol established by Polymer Scope. The procedure used a 1:1 ratio by weight of iron(II) sulfate to iron(III) chloride that were stirred together in a large beaker of deionized water. Ammonium hydroxide was added dropwise to decrease the pH of the solution and result in the formation of magnetite nanoparticles as a precipitate. This initial method was unsuccessful and formed maghemite ($Fe_2$$O_3$).

The second method of synthesis was a modification of the first method that was successful and utilized a different base and chemical ratios based on other conventional synthesis procedures [@ali_synthesis_2016]. The procedure involved using a 1:2 ratio by volume of 0.326M iron(II) sulfate to 0.326M iron(III) chloride. These were stirred together in an equal volume of deionized water at a speed of 450 RPM. After the solution homogenized a 1.9:1 ratio of 0.44M sodium hydroxide to total solution was added dropwise until the pH is greater than 9. Following this a magnet was used to separate the magnetite nanoparticles from the solution and the powder was rinsed multiple times with ethanol and deionized water (@Figure_2).

### Nanoparticle Characterization
##### Dynamic Light Scattering
A dynamic light scattering instrument was designed to approximate the size of the nanoparticles. It connects the relationship between particle size and the particle speed as a result of Brownian Motion, the random motion of all particles caused by collisions between molecules and thermal energy. The setup utilizes a laser that hits the particles and photodiodes at three angles (15°, 90°, and 165°) to measure the change in scatter of the light to analyze the motion of the particles. The instrument was designed based on the OpenDLS open-source project [@noauthor_opendls_nodate]. The design and hardware was modified to utilize input regarding the scatter from multiple angles. The instrument was designed with 3D printed parts, a laser, photodiodes, a circuit on a breadboard, and Arduino Uno R3, and a program to analyze data collected from the photodiodes (@Figure_3, @Figure_4). The photoresist is used to measure the change in scatter as a result of Brownian Motion. That data is then sent to the Arduino Uno R3 after the signals are amplified by a circuit (@Figure_5). The data is read and analyzed on a computer using an autocorrelation function that extracts information on the time dependence of the signal with the presence of noise. The information from the autocorrelation function is utilized to determine the diffusion coefficient. The Diffusion coefficient along with other known variables can be plugged into the Stokes-Einstein equation. The code used was a modified version of the OpenDLS project that was altered for the properties of Iron Oxide Magnetite nanoparticles suspended in ethanol along with taking input from multiple different angles and combining the data collected and outputting an approximate particle size [@noauthor_opendls_nodate].

#### UV-Vis Spectrophotometry
A UV-Vis spectrophotometer was used to collect optical spectrum data on the nanoparticles. For this 0.1g of magnetite powder was added to 80mL of ethanol and the solution was put into the UV-Vis spectrophotometer after stirring the solution. The absorbance was measured across a spectral range from 190nm to 1100nm. Multiple samples of the same nanoparticles were tested to ensure precision and increase the accuracy of the data collected. This data was analyzed along with the dynamic light scattering instrument’s data in order to ensure consistency and some degree of accuracy.
### Simulating Magnetic Hyperthermia
#### Fabricating Phantom Tissue
Thermochromic phantom tissue was fabricated to simulate magnetic hyperthermia with a color changing gel designed to change color with heat and mimic human tissue. A 1:2 ratio by volume of glycerol to deionized water was used. They were stirred with a magnetic stirrer at 350 RPM while being heated to 90℃. Once the temperature reached 90℃, agar powder and a little thermochromic powder were added and stirred for 5 more minutes. The contents were transferred to a mold container and refrigerated until they were necessary to test the procedure (@Figure_6).

#### Alternating Magnetic Field Testing
For testing generating an alternating magnetic field, the conventional methods involve a coil and AC current which is what was initially tested. That method was unsuccessful due to the power sources not being able to provide a sufficient level of current to generate a strong enough magnetic field. An alternative method was utilized involving a high speed motor and neodymium magnets that were placed in alternating directions in a circular fashion within a 3D printed setup (@Figure_7). Phantom tissue was then placed into test tubes and 0.01g of magnetite powder in 0.05M sodium citrate were injected into the phantom tissue and held near the rotating magnetic field setup using a chemistry stand and clamp (@Figure_8). A spirit thermometer was used to measure the temperature over time instead of an electronic sensor as the alternating magnetic fields were messing with the measurement outputs from any electric sensors nearby.

## Conclusion
This study has been able to successfully develop a low-cost method of performing magnetic hyperthermia from start to finish (starting with particle synthesis and finishing with testing). Overall, the budget used for the procedure amounted to less than $200, with the rest of the equipment used being standard equipment that can be found in a physics or chemistry research lab like a UV-Vis spectrophotometer and a 24V power supply.

A repeatable procedure was developed for iron oxide magnetite nanoparticle synthesis. It has the capability of size adjustment by simply altering the amount of base added during the coprecipitation synthesis procedure. There is evidence that it did alter the properties of the nanoparticles as shown by both the UV-Vis spectrums and the size measurements from the dynamic light scattering instrument although more testing or expensive characterization equipment would be necessary to accurately quantify an exact particle size.

For nanoparticle characterization, a low-cost Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) instrument was developed and proved to have precision in outputting consistent results for multiple samples from the same synthesis batch. The relative information between the UV-Vis spectrums regarding the particles having slightly different properties were consistent, but additional testing involving more expensive equipment like an electron microscope would be necessary to make conclusions on the instrument’s accuracy and the exact size of the nanoparticles.

For magnetic hyperthermia testing and simulation, thermochromic phantom tissue was successfully fabricated to mimic human tissue for the purpose of simulation. The final setup was able to generate a temperature change, although further testing is necessary to make further conclusions and understand the inconsistencies in the temperature measurements. There are many possibilities as to why the temperatures were inconsistent with the main factor being the low frequency of the alternating magnetic field which resulted in particle clumping when simulating the procedure. The clinically accepted optimal frequency is 100-400 kHz but due to resource limitations.

This study establishes a low-cost method that can be utilized for magnetic hyperthermia simulation and experimentation.

### Future Research and Directions
This system establishes a functional baseline for the low-cost experimental investigation of Magnetic Hyperthermia parameters that allows for the easy variation of multiple variables. That allows for a lot of future experimentation to be performed utilizing this experimentation setup including more testing to experimentally investigate the effects of factors like nanoparticle size, shape, magnetic field strength, and alternating magnetic field frequency have on Specific Absorption Rates (SAR). That data can be compared to and combined with quantitative theoretical calculations to assess the connection between the variables and optimize the outcomes while taking into account patient safety. Additional more effective methods of simulation with phantom tissue that provide better visual results is another area for exploration. Along with that, this project has introduced an unconventional approach to magnetic hyperthermia using rotating magnetic fields. There is more research to be done on the comparison of the performance of rotating magnetic fields to alternating electromagnetic fields that considers the effectiveness of each procedure in addition to safety concerns associated with each of them. This project establishes a strong foundation and allows for a lot more experimentation to be done in future to optimize the procedure of magnetic hyperthermia for both optimal performance and minimal health related hazards.
