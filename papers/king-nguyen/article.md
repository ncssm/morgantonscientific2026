---
title: "Do Sweeteners Alter the Effects of Matcha? The Study of Natural versus Artificial Sweeteners on the Cognitive and Locomotive Activity of Drosophila melanogaster"
short_title: "Do Sweeteners Alter the Effects of Matcha?"
abstract:
    This study investigated whether the type of sweetener, natural (honey) or artificial (Splenda), alters the physiological and behavioral benefits of matcha green tea in *Drosophila melanogaster*. Over a 10-day experimental period, flies were administered dietary inclusions of matcha in combination with either honey or Splenda. This study assessed survival rates, body weight, locomotor performance, and cognitive performance (learning and memory). Results indicated that while survival and memory were not significantly impacted across groups, the high-sugar honey diet supplemented with matcha revealed a significantly lower locomotor performance compared to other dietary treatments. These findings suggest that the benefits of matcha do not alter the negative effects of a high-sugar diet. This study highlights the importance of dietary context when evaluating the health-promoting aspect of functional foods.
---
## Introduction
Sweeteners are widely consumed dietary compounds that interact with taste receptors to enhance the perception of sweetness and improve palatability of foods and beverages [@fernstrom_mechanisms_2012]. They play a significant role in modern diets, particularly in drinks that are marketed as functional or health-promoting [@mroczek_effect_2023]. Sweeteners are generally categorized into two groups: nutritive sweeteners, such as sucrose and honey, and non-nutritive or artificial sweeteners, such as sucralose. Nutritive sweeteners provide caloric energy from natural sources, whereas non-nutritive sweeteners provide little to no caloric value and are usually derived from plant extracts or created through chemical synthesis [@oehler_sweeteners:_2020]. While these compounds are often used to improve taste and reduce caloric intake, their biological effects extend beyond taste perception and may influence metabolic and neurological processes [@pepino_metabolic_2015]. 

Excessive consumption of dietary sugars have been associated with negative health outcomes, including metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, and impaired cognitive function [@ma_excessive_2022]. As a result, artificial sweeteners have been increasingly adopted as alternatives to reduce sugar intake [@sharma_artificial_2016]. However, the long-term safety and physiological effects of artificial sweeteners remain controversial, particularly with respect to their impact on neural function, behavior, and memory [@goncalves_association_2025]. Given the widespread consumption of both nutritive and artificial sweeteners, understanding how these compounds affect cognitive and behavioral outcomes is an important area of study [@pepino_metabolic_2015]. 

Matcha, a powdered form of green tea derived from *Camellia sinensis*, has gained substantial popularity as a functional beverage due to its perceived health benefits [@kochman_health_2020]. Although matcha and green tea originate from the same plant species, their cultivation and preparation methods differ substantially [@jakubczyk_exploring_2024]. Matcha is produced from tea leaves that are shaded prior to harvest, a process that increases chlorophyll content, L-theanine levels, and overall nutrient density [@jakubczyk_exploring_2024]. The leaves are then steamed, de-veined, and stone-ground into a fine powder, allowing consumers to ingest the entire leaf rather than an aqueous extract [@jakubczyk_exploring_2024]. As a result, matcha contains high concentrations of bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and catechins, which have been associated with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and potential cognitive benefits [@jakubczyk_exploring_2024]. 

Despite increasing interest in matcha’s health-promoting properties, it is rarely consumed in isolation [@dreher_food_2018]. In many popular beverages, including matcha lattes and sweetened teas, matcha is combined with milk and various sweeteners, both natural and artificial [@dreher_food_2018]. While the beneficial effects of green tea and its associated compounds have been extensively studied, considerably less research has focused specifically on matcha. Furthermore, limited attention has been given to how matcha’s effects may be altered when consumed alongside sweeteners. It remains unclear whether matcha’s potential cognitive and behavioral benefits are preserved, enhanced, or diminished when combined with different types of sweeteners. 

Model organisms provide an effective approach for investigating dietary effects on physiological and neurological outcomes [@banerjee_using_2020]. The fruit fly, *Drosophila melanogaster*, is a well-established model organism in biological and biomedical research due to its short lifespan, well-characterized nervous system, and high degree of genetic conservation with humans [@banerjee_using_2020]. Many genes and signaling pathways involved in metabolism, neural function, and behavior are conserved between *Drosophila* and humans, with studies showing that >70% of human disease-causing genes are conserved in *Drosophila*, making fruit flies a suitable model for studying diet-related effects on memory and locomotor activity [@banerjee_using_2020; @pandey_human_2011]. Additionally, validated behavioral assays allow for the assessment of cognitive performance and movement in response to dietary treatments [@nichols_methods_2012].

This study aims to investigate the effects of matcha and sweetener supplementation on memory and locomotor behavior using *Drosophila melanogaster* as a model system. Specifically, this study examines whether matcha alone or matcha combined with natural or artificial sweeteners differentially influences behavioral outcomes. Due to the short duration of this experiment and the constraints of a four-week course, this study is exploratory in nature and is not intended to produce statistically significant conclusions. Rather, it seeks to identify trends and generate hypotheses that may inform future, longer-term investigations. 

Given the increasing popularity of matcha-based beverages and the widespread use of sweeteners in their preparation, understanding how these components interact is relevant to both scientific research and information about public health. By examining the combined effects of matcha and sweeteners on memory and locomotor activity in a fruit fly model, this study aims to provide insight into how functional beverages may influence neurological and behavioral outcomes. 

We hypothesized that matcha supplementation would improve memory and locomotor performance in *Drosophila melanogaster*, and that these beneficial effects would be able to reduce the negative effects associated with a high-sugar diet. These effects would increase when matcha is combined with a natural sweetener, such as honey, rather than with an artificial sweetener, such as Splenda. 

## Methods

### Chemicals and reagents

The experimental materials consisted of sucralose sweetener packets (Splenda), 100% pure honey (Virginia Brand), universal quality matcha (Maeda-en), tea tree pure essential oil (Nature’s Truth), Formula 4-24 Instant Drosophila Medium (Carolina Biological Supply Company), and Active Dry Yeast Original (Fleischmann's). All solutions were prepared using tap water. 

### Fruit fly (*Drosophila melanogaster*) stock culture

A mixed population of wild-type *Drosophila melanogaster* and *Drosophila hydei*, sourced from PetSmart (Morganton, NC, USA), was used. 

### Experimental design

48 flies were divided into 6 groups, with each group containing 4 males and 4 females, all randomly selected from stock flies. Base mediums were made with 15 grams of potato flakes, 0.03g of yeast, and 15mL of the respective solution. The concentrations of honey and sucralose were standardized to approximate the sweetness intensity of 10% sucrose solution to maintain consistency and accuracy in measurements. The 0.7% matcha solution was determined based on previous findings regarding the physiological effects of green tea on *Drosophila*, [@lopez_impact_2016]. Group 1 was fed a base medium with tap water. Group 2-3 were fed dietary inclusions of sugar-supplemented water, with one group containing 6% honey and one 1.2% Splenda. Group 4 was fed base medium with 0.7% matcha. Groups 5-6 were fed dietary inclusions of sugar-supplemented 0.7% matcha concentrations, with one group containing 6% honey and one containing 1.2% Splenda. Flies were kept on these dietary treatments for a 10-day duration, with media being replaced every 3-4 days with freshly made concentrations to ensure nutritional value was maintained and prevent contamination.  

### Fly survival rate assessment

The survival of the flies were recorded in 24-hour intervals for the duration of the 10 day study. At the conclusion of the 10-day period, the survival rate for each treatment group was calculated and expressed as a percentage of the total fly population.

### Measurement of locomotor performance by negative geotaxis test

Locomotor performance was evaluated every 48 hours using a negative geotaxis assay. For each trial, flies were transferred from their respective vials into a sterilized, cylindrical tube without the use of anesthesia to avoid potential impacts on motor function. Flies were tested for movement by tapping the tube moderately 3 times to ensure that the flies began at the bottom. The flies that successfully climbed past the 10cm threshold within a 30-second interval were then recorded. This procedure was repeated for all groups with 5 trials per group. 

### Memory retention assay

Memory retention was determined by using a Y-maze associative learning protocol and conditioning the flies to associate light with a negative stimulus [@aoki_y-maze_2015]. Y-maze was 3D- printed. This procedure was conducted in 4 day intervals. The assay utilized the repellent properties of tea-tree oil (Melaleuca alternifolia) to condition flies against their natural preference for light [@klauck_insecticidal_2014; @kain_phototactic_2012]. Prior to the experiment a control trial was carried out to confirm that there is no inherent bias for any specific side within the maze. The Y-maze was oriented vertically due to their negative geotaxis, an innate response to move upwards [@gargano_rapid_2005]. During conditioning trials, one arm contained a light source with tea tree oil and the other arm contained no scent or light. Three conditioning trials were conducted in order to condition the flies to associate light with a negative stimulus. Each trial lasted 2 minutes, and flies were given a one-minute rest period under uniform light. This procedure was conducted 3 times. After the assay, the flies were placed back into their respective vials. Memory retention was assessed 3 hours later, the tea-tree oil was removed and flies were introduced to the Y-maze with one arm containing light and the other no light. It was then observed whether the flies remembered and retained the association of the light with the repellent scent. 
2.7. Data Analysis 

All statistical analysis was carried out using Graph Pad Prism 10. The results were expressed as the standard error of mean. Two-way ANOVA (or mixed model), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used to analyze the results, followed by T-test, one \* indicates P ≤ 0.05, two \*\* P ≤ 0.01, three \*\*\* P ≤ 0.001, and four \*\*\*\* P ≤ 0.0001. 

## Results

Control flies (water-fed) exhibited 100% survival rate throughout the duration of the study. While descriptive observations suggested a decline in survival for certain groups, specifically flies fed matcha-supplemented with honey, a Two-way ANOVA revealed no statistically significant differences in survival across all treatment groups (p > 0.05). Under the conditions tested, neither matcha, Splenda, nor honey significantly impacted fly survival rates compared to control (water-fed) flies within the 10-day study period (p > 0.05).

## Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to examine how matcha supplementation, alone and in combination with natural and artificial sweeteners, influences survival, body weight, locomotor performance, and short-memory retention in *Drosophila melanogaster*. This question addresses a notable gap in the current literature, as most studies examining the cognitive and behavioral effects of tea-derived compounds focus on green tea extracts rather than matcha specifically. These studies also rarely consider how these effects may be altered when consumed alongside sweeteners commonly used in functional beverages. Given the widespread consumption of sweetened matcha products, understanding how sweetener type interacts with matcha is not only biologically relevant, but also timely.

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that dietary composition significantly influences multiple aspects in *D. melanogaster*, and that the effects of matcha are not uniform across all measured variables. While matcha supplementation was associated with relatively stable body weight and short-term memory retention, it did not consistently improve survival or locomotor performance. Moreover, the type of sweetener paired with matcha played a critical role in shaping these outcomes, with honey and Splenda producing distinct and sometimes opposing effects. 

Survival analysis revealed that flies maintained on the water-only control diet exhibited 100% survival over the experimental period, while sugar-supplemented diets were generally associated with increased mortality. Contrary to the initial hypothesis, matcha supplementation did not universally improve survival outcomes. In particular, flies fed matcha combined with honey exhibited the lowest survival rate by the end of the study period. One possible explanation is that the combination of matcha and honey increased caloric intake or metabolic demand beyond a tolerable threshold, leading to reduced survival. An important note is that this experiment looked at the effects of high-sugar diets (HSD) alongside matcha, so the fruit flies were exposed to an increased amount of sweetener, above normal amounts. Previous studies have shown that high-sugar diets can accelerate metabolic dysregulation and reduce lifespan in *Drosophila* [@moon_modeling_2026], which may override any protective effects associated with matcha’s bioactive compounds. 

Body weight data support this interpretation, as honey-containing diets were associated with higher average body weights, particularly at mid-to-late experimental time points. Increased body weight in *Drosophila* has been linked to altered lipid storage and metabolic stress [@subramanian_altered_2013], which could contribute to reduced survival. In contrast, flies fed matcha with Splenda maintained consistently low body weight despite being exposed to a high-sugar diet, suggesting that non-nutritive sweeteners may influence energy balance differently than caloric sugars. These findings highlight that changes in body weight and survival are not necessarily coupled and may reflect distinct physiological processes. While fluctuations in body mass were observed between Day 3 and Day 10, these results must be interpreted with caution. It should be noted that the experimental groups originated from various stocks, resulting in minor variations in baseline weight on Day 1. To account for the initial differences on the final analysis, weight gain and loss were evaluated.  The average weights of dietary groups may be skewed by the inherent sexual dimorphism of *Drosophila melanogaster*; the death of a single female with a higher body mass can significantly lower the group average. Furthermore, the sudden spike in weight change recorded on Day 9, followed by an immediate correction on Day 10, suggests a potential source error. This could be due to the sensitivity of the analytical scale or external environmental interference during the weighing process, rather than the actual weight shift of the dietary group [@millington_sex_2018]. 

Locomotor performance, assessed through negative geotaxis, declined most prominently in flies fed matcha-containing diets, particularly when combined with sweeteners. This finding was unexpected, as green tea polyphenols and L-theanine have been associated with neuroprotective effects in other systems [@chen_neuroprotective_2018]. One possible explanation is that matcha’s bioactive compounds may differentially affect neural circuits involved in motor coordination versus those involved in memory [@sokary_therapeutic_2023]. Alternatively, the observed decline in climbing ability could be a reflection of increased fatigue, altered muscle metabolism, a correlation to their age, or reduced motivation rather than direct neuromuscular impairment [@rhodenizer_genetic_2008]. 

The memory retention assay was the most complicated test to perform on the fruit flies. Due to the limitations regarding the duration of this experiment, the memory assay was only able to be performed once. This study utilized a 3D-printed Y-maze and tea tree oil as a repellent as it allowed for reliable conditioning without inducing fly mortality, which had occurred with peppermint extract during preliminary trials. Regarding the data collected, flies fed matcha-containing diets exhibited higher memory indices immediately following conditioning, indicating successful learning of the light-repellent association. After a two-hour retention interval, flies fed matcha alone maintained high memory indices, which suggests that matcha supplementation may support short-term memory retention. 

However, this effect was strongly modulated by sweetener type. While flies fed matcha with Splenda showed a decline in memory retention, the most pronounced impairment was observed in the matcha with honey group, which exhibited a negative memory index. This indicates not only a loss of the learned association but a reversal in behavioral preference. One possible explanation is that honey-induced metabolic stress interfered with neural processes underlying memory consolidation or retrieval. HSD has been shown to impair learning and memory in *Drosophila* through mechanisms involving insulin signaling and oxidative stress [@murashov_preference_2021]. Previous studies have also shown that HSD alters food-odor associations, disrupting associative learning [@yapici_eating_2023]. 

Notably, this study assessed immediate post-conditioning memory for the 0 hour data by taking the average of the conditioning trials. By using this method, the short-term memory of the flies was able to be displayed, as the original graph for this data would simply show a 0 memory index percentage. Unlike memory retention assays that assess immediate post-conditioning memory, this study included a pre-conditioning control to verify that there was an absence of innate light and dark bias. During this, there was no scent, only a light and dark side within the Y-maze. By averaging out the conditioning trials, rather than only showing a baseline preference, it ensures that observed memory indices reflected learned behavior.

Regular re-feeding every 3-4 days was also critical, as matcha, when exposed to light, heat, and oxygen, oxidizes. When oxidation occurs, it can lead to a loss of flavor and quality along with its rich, green color [@phuah_review_2023]. During this study, it was observed that after 3-4 days the matcha medium went from a vibrant green to a dull green. Re-fooding helped maintain consistency in dietary exposure across the experimental period. 

The sex of the fruit flies was relevant to this experiment as green tea polyphenols have been found to extend the lifespan of male *Drosophila melanogaster* [@lopez_green_2014]. However, no consistent sex-specific trends were observed in this study. It is also noteworthy that egg-laying and larval development were observed in the water with the honey treatment group during the experimental period. Although reproductive output was not quantified in this study, increased reproductive activity may have influenced energy allocation, survival, or behavioral outcomes in this group and should be considered when interpreting these results. 

Throughout all assays conducted, groups with honey (6%) supplementation seemed to have a more observable and statistically difference compared to control and other experimental groups, while no noticeable changes were observed for Splenda-supplemented (1.2%) groups. Since artificial sweeteners like Splenda have no caloric value, they cannot be broken down by the digestive system [@m_exploring_2024]. Studies have shown that consuming artificial sweeteners long-term can have a negative impact, but short-term consumption has shown no significant risks [@sharma_artificial_2016]. This would help explain why during the 10-day dietary intervention the high sugar diet with supplementation of honey has more negative effects compared to splenda, since honey is a natural sugar it can be broken down by the digestive system and has a caloric intake [@seraglio_current_2021]. When consuming such a high amount of sugar the blood concentration would most likely be higher compared to those fed with Splenda supplementation [@akhbari_effects_2021].

### Limitations and Future Work

In this study, neither the survivability analysis nor the Y-maze memory assay reaches statistical significance (p > 0.05). This is likely due to the combination of a limited sample size (n=8) and only a single-interval testing window for the Y-maze conditioning assay, with these constraints there is a chance that subtle dietary impacts on memory may have been missed. 

While the 10-day dietary intervention provided a few insights into the effects of matcha and sweeteners in *Drosophila melanogaster*, this duration may not have been sufficient to observe long-term physiological changes induced by these dietary treatments. This observation window only represents a fraction of the *Drosophila* adult lifespan, therefore the results observed here may represent how flies first react to the diet, rather than the long-term effects of consuming it. In addition, the small sample size limits statistical power of the study and restricts conclusions to observed trends rather than definitive effects. The use of mixed fly species (*D. melanogaster* and *D. hydei*) may have also introduced variability in behavioral responses.  

To build upon these current findings, future studies should utilize larger cohorts and genetically homogenous fly populations over extended exposure periods to produce more statistically significant results. Investigating molecular markers, specifically oxidative stress, insulin signaling, neurotransmitter activity, or glycogen and trehalose levels would help give a better insight on the biological changes underlying behavioral shifts. Furthermore, exploring specific dose-dependent effects of matcha and various sweeteners instead of just a broad high-sugar diet would provide greater insight by enabling finer resolution of threshold and interaction effects. Given prior evidence that green tea polyphenols resulted in a decrease in female reproductive output and impacted the reproductive organs in both male and female flies [@lopez_impact_2016], incorporating courtship assays and sex-specific analyses would be informative alongside proper equipment and software for monitoring. 

## Conclusion

This study has shown that the benefits of matcha does not alter the negative effects of high-sugar honey diet in *Drosophila melanogaster*. While matcha alone showed trends consistent with preserved memory retention and stable physiological outcomes, these effects were not maintained when matcha was combined with a high-sugar honey diet. Flies fed a high-sugar diet supplemented with honey exhibited increased body weight, reduced locomotor performance, and impaired memory retention, suggesting that excess dietary sugar may counteract or override the potential benefits associated with matcha’s bioactive compounds. In contrast, matcha combined with Splenda, produced few observable changes and no statistical significance was found across conducted assays, indicating that non-nutritive sweeteners, specifically Splenda, did not have much of an effect on fruit flies during the 10-day dietary intervention. This study highlights the importance of dietary context when evaluating the health-promoting properties of functional foods.
