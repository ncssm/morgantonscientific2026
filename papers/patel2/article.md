---
title: Analysis of Wolbachia Presence in Pyrrharctia and Boisea (Woolly Worms and Boxelder Bugs)
abstract: |
    About six out of ten bug types globally carry a bacterium called *Wolbachia*. While *Wolbachia* can change how insects reproduce and stop viruses from spreading, we still know little about how it shows up locally. This study investigated the prevalence of *Wolbachia* in two distinct arthropod species from Burke County, North Carolina: boxelder bugs, *Boisea rubrolineata* Hemiptera, and woolly worms, *Pyrrharctia isabella* Lepidoptera. Using molecular detection methods on specimens collected from the NCSSM Morganton campus, DNA extracts were analyzed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with arthropod-specific COI primers for species confirmation and *Wolbachia*-specific 16S rRNA primers to detect infection. Gel electrophoresis indicated that 4 out of 5 boxelder bug samples tested positive for *Wolbachia*, suggesting an 80% infection rate. However, 2 out of 2 woolly worm samples exhibited faint yet detectable *Wolbachia* bands, suggesting a 100% infection rate. Species identity was confirmed through DNA sequencing submitted to the Wolbachia Project, with quality scores (Q20) ranging from 0.96 to 0.97. These results provide preliminary evidence that *Wolbachia* infection may be common in these species and contribute to the bacterium's broader ecological presence across arthropod orders in the local ecosystem.
---
## Introduction

*Wolbachia* is a genus of intracellular bacteria first discovered in 1936 by Marshall Hertig and S. Burt Wolbach in the reproductive tissues of mosquitoes [@hertig1924studies]. These maternally inherited endosymbionts have since been recognized as one of the most widespread parasites in the animal kingdom, infecting an estimated 66% of all arthropod species worldwide (@hilgenboecker2008many; @zug2012host). *Wolbachia* is primarily found in the reproductive tissues of its hosts. It manipulates host reproduction through cytoplasmic incompatibility, parthenogenesis induction, feminization, and male killing (werren2008wolbachia). *Wolbachia* has also been shown to alter host immune systems and metabolic pathways, providing protection against RNA viruses such as dengue, Zika, and chikungunya [@moreira2009wolbachia].

*Wolbachia* has become increasingly prominent in the world of public health. Certain strains of *Wolbachia* can reduce an insect vector's ability to transmit viruses to humans (johnson2015impact). This discovery has led to the intentional introduction of *Wolbachia* into mosquito populations as a control strategy, limiting the spread of diseases [@iturbe2011wolbachia]. Beyond scientists, organizations like The World Mosquito Program have successfully deployed *Wolbachia*-infected mosquitoes in many countries, reducing dengue transmission [@denton2022shipments].

Although there is extensive research on the implications of *Wolbachia* in mosquito-borne diseases, the distribution and prevalence of *Wolbachia* across different arthropod taxonomic groups are still vague. Previous studies have documented *Wolbachia* infection in other insect orders like Hemiptera (true bugs) and Lepidoptera (butterflies and moths), with infection rates varying considerably between and within taxonomic groups (@rasgon2004phylogenetic; @ahmed2016horizontal). In Hemiptera, multiple species have been reported to be infected with *Wolbachia* [@rasgon2004phylogenetic]. Similarly, Lepidoptera have been documented to have horizontal transmission events, demonstrating the complexity of the infection [@ahmed2016horizontal].

Additionally, *Wolbachia* research at the local scale is limited. Understanding the distribution of *Wolbachia* across different arthropod taxonomic groups in specific areas can provide insight into transmission dynamics and the role *Wolbachia* plays in local ecosystems. Furthermore, investigating infection rates among distantly related orders can help dictate if *Wolbachia* presence differs at the taxonomic level or at the population and species level. Despite this, no prior study has characterized *Wolbachia* prevalence in *Boisea rubrolineata* or *Pyrrharctia isabella* specifically, leaving a gap in the literature on infection dynamics across taxonomically divergent orders at the local scale.

This study investigates the presence of *Wolbachia* in two arthropod species commonly found in Burke County, North Carolina: boxelder bugs (*Boisea rubrolineata*) from the order Hemiptera and woolly worms (*Pyrrharctia isabella*) from the order Lepidoptera. These species were selected due to their local abundance and their distinct insect orders with different reproductive strategies. We hypothesized that *Wolbachia* would be present in both species, consistent with the bacterium's widespread distribution. This study used techniques such as DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, and gel electrophoresis to detect *Wolbachia* infection and compare prevalence rates between these two taxonomically divergent groups. The results of the study contribute to our understanding of the ecological distribution of *Wolbachia* and provide baseline data for future research regarding *Wolbachia* diversity in this region. By documenting *Wolbachia* infection in two taxonomically divergent species from the same locality, this study contributes baseline data for understanding the ecological and taxonomic drivers of *Wolbachia* distribution.

## Methods

### Sample Collection

Boxelder bugs (*Boisea rubrolineata*) and woolly worms (*Pyrrharctia isabella*) were collected from distinct locations on the NCSSM Morganton campus in Burke County, North Carolina. Boxelder bugs were collected from the exterior of Goodwin Hall, the main administrative building on campus with no residential occupancy, while woolly worms were collected from the grounds surrounding the Residence Hall, a student dormitory. These distinct collection sites ensured sampling across different microhabitats. Specimens were immediately placed in separate sterile containers by species to prevent cross-contamination. All specimens were preserved in 95% ethanol and frozen until DNA extraction. A total of five boxelder bug specimens and two woolly worm specimens were collected for molecular analysis.

### DNA Extraction

DNA was extracted from each specimen using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol with minor modifications for arthropod tissue. Due to the larger body size of woolly worms compared to boxelder bugs, abdominal tissue was dissected from woolly worm specimens to access tissue with high DNA concentration. For both species, individual specimens were placed in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and broken down using a sterile pestle in 180 μL of ATL buffer. Next, 20 μL of proteinase K was added to these samples. Then, 200 μL of Buffer AL was added with new tips. These samples were then vortexed for 10 seconds to ensure thorough mixing of tissue and lysis buffer. Following homogenization, samples were incubated at 56°C for 1 hour to allow complete tissue digestion. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 30 seconds to pellet cellular debris. The supernatant containing DNA was transferred to new tubes.

### DNA Purification

After the DNA was extracted, 4 spin columns with flow-through tubes were labeled by sample. The supernatant was then transferred into the corresponding spin column and placed in a centrifuge to spin for 1 minute at 8,000 rpm. 500 μL of Buffer AW1 was then placed into each spin column and centrifuged again for 1 minute at 8,000 rpm. The liquid from the bottom was then discarded. Next, 500 μL of Buffer AW2 was added to the same spin columns and centrifuged for 3 minutes at 14,000 rpm.

### DNA Elution

4 new microcentrifuge tubes were labeled with their corresponding samples, and their corresponding spin columns were placed in these tubes. With new pipette tips for each sample, 100 μL of Buffer AE was added to each spin column. After resting at room temperature for approximately 1 minute, these tubes were placed in the centrifuge for 1 minute at 8,000 rpm, pulling DNA through the spin column into the microcentrifuge tubes. These tubes were then stored in the freezer.

### Polymerase Chain Reaction

Two separate PCR assays were performed on each DNA sample to verify DNA quality and detect *Wolbachia* presence. The first PCR used arthropod-specific cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) primers to confirm successful DNA extraction and provide material for species identification. The second PCR employed *Wolbachia*-specific 16S rRNA primers to detect potential *Wolbachia* infection.

For the arthropod COI PCR, each reaction contained PCR buffer, MgCl₂, dNTPs, forward and reverse primers, Taq DNA polymerase, and template DNA in a standard reaction volume. The thermocycling protocol included an initial denaturation step, followed by cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension, with a final extension period. For the *Wolbachia* 16S rRNA PCR, reaction components and thermocycling conditions were identical to those used for the arthropod COI amplification. Both reactions were performed in a thermal cycler, and successful amplification was confirmed through gel electrophoresis.

### Gel Electrophoresis

PCR products from both arthropod COI and *Wolbachia* 16S amplifications were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis. Two separate 1.5% agarose gels were prepared: one for boxelder bug samples and one for woolly worm samples. This separation prevented potential cross-contamination and allowed for clearer visual comparison within each species group. Each gel was prepared by dissolving agarose powder in Tris-Borate-EDTA buffer. 7 μL of each PCR product was mixed with loading dye and loaded into individual wells. A 100 bp DNA ladder was loaded in the first lane of each gel to allow size estimation of amplified products. The lanes were then loaded alternating between the arthropod PCR product and the *Wolbachia* product for each sample, and ended with controls.

### DNA Sequencing and Species Confirmation

To confirm species identity beyond morphological identification, PCR products from the arthropod COI amplification were submitted to the Wolbachia Project for Sanger sequencing. Both forward and reverse primers were used for bidirectional sequencing to ensure accuracy. Returned sequences were analyzed for quality using Q20 scores, and sequences with Q20 scores above 0.95 were considered high quality. Species identification was performed by comparing obtained sequences against the GenBank nucleotide database using BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool).

## Results

### Species Confirmation

Although specimens were initially identified using the iNaturalist platform, species identity was further confirmed through DNA sequencing of the arthropod COI gene region. PCR products amplified using arthropod-specific primers were sent to the Wolbachia Project for Sanger sequencing. High-quality sequences were obtained for representative specimens from both species, indicating high accuracy of base calling.

For boxelder bug specimens, the aligned sequence returned a definitive match to *Boisea rubrolineata* voucher specimens, confirming the species identification. For woolly worm specimens, the aligned sequence showed a definitive match to *Pyrrharctia isabella* voucher specimens. These molecular identifications validated the initial morphological classifications and ensured that subsequent *Wolbachia* detection results could be confidently attributed to the correct host species.

### *Wolbachia* Detection in Boxelder Bugs

Gel electrophoresis of PCR products from boxelder bug samples revealed successful amplification for both arthropod COI and *Wolbachia* 16S primers (@fig4). All 5 boxelder bug samples showed strong, clear bands for the arthropod COI control, confirming that DNA extraction was successful and that PCR conditions were appropriate. This positive control result validated the reliability of subsequent *Wolbachia* detection.

For *Wolbachia*-specific amplification, 4 out of 5 boxelder bug samples displayed visible bands at the expected size for the 16S rRNA gene product (@fig4). These bands indicated the presence of *Wolbachia* DNA in those specimens. The intensity of the *Wolbachia* bands varied slightly among positive samples but was clearly distinguishable from the background in all 4 cases. One boxelder bug sample showed no detectable *Wolbachia* band despite successful arthropod COI amplification, indicating either absence of *Wolbachia* infection in that individual or infection levels below the detection threshold of this assay. The overall infection rate in boxelder bugs was therefore 80% (4 out of 5 specimens).

### *Wolbachia* Detection in Woolly Worms

Gel electrophoresis of PCR products from woolly worm samples also demonstrated successful amplification of arthropod COI primers in both specimens, confirming adequate DNA quality and PCR functionality (@fig5). Both woolly worm samples showed strong bands for the arthropod control, indicating that the DNA extraction protocol was effective. For *Wolbachia* detection, both woolly worm samples exhibited bands in the *Wolbachia* 16S lane. However, these bands were noticeably fainter than those observed in most boxelder bug samples (@fig5). The first woolly worm sample produced a faint but detectable band, while the second sample displayed a slightly stronger band. Despite the reduced band intensity, both samples produced bands above background levels consistent with *Wolbachia* amplification. The infection rate in woolly worms was therefore 100% (2 out of 2 specimens).

## Discussion

This study detected *Wolbachia* infection in both boxelder bugs (*Boisea rubrolineata*) and woolly worms (*Pyrrharctia isabella*), two arthropod species representing distinct taxonomic orders found in Burke County, North Carolina. Although *Wolbachia* has been extensively studied globally, relatively few studies have examined infection prevalence at small geographic scales. The infection rate was 80% in boxelder bugs (4 out of 5 individuals) and 100% in woolly worms (2 out of 2 individuals), although these values should be interpreted as preliminary prevalence estimates due to the limited sample size. These findings are consistent with the well-established observation that *Wolbachia* is a widespread endosymbiont across arthropod taxa, infecting diverse insect orders including Hemiptera and Lepidoptera [@zug2012host].

The high prevalence of *Wolbachia* in boxelder bugs aligns with previous research documenting *Wolbachia* infection in other hemipteran species. Large-scale analyses have estimated that approximately 66% of terrestrial arthropod species may be infected with *Wolbachia* [@hilgenboecker2008many], whereas more recent analyses using expanded datasets suggest a lower estimate of roughly 40% [@zug2012host]. The prevalence observed in boxelder bugs in this study falls within the higher end of these reported estimates. Rasgon and Scott (2004) reported *Wolbachia* presence in bed bugs (*Cimex lectularius*) and bat bugs (*Oeciacus vicarius*), both members of Hemiptera, suggesting that this endosymbiont may be common across the order. The current study extends this finding to *Boisea rubrolineata*, adding to the growing body of evidence that *Wolbachia* infection is prevalent in true bugs. The detection of *Wolbachia* in woolly worms similarly supports previous findings in Lepidoptera. Ahmed et al. (2016) documented evidence for horizontal transmission of *Wolbachia* among butterflies and moths, indicating active spread of the bacterium within this order. The present results suggest that *Wolbachia* may be maintained in *Pyrrharctia isabella* populations through vertical maternal transmission, horizontal transfer, or both mechanisms.

The presence of *Wolbachia* in both species from different insect orders is consistent with previous findings that this endosymbiont occurs across diverse arthropod taxa.

### Faint *Wolbachia* Bands

The faint *Wolbachia* bands observed in woolly worm samples may reflect biological or technical factors affecting amplification efficiency. While both woolly worm samples tested positive for *Wolbachia*, the bands detected on the gel were noticeably fainter than those observed in most boxelder bug samples. Several explanations could account for this difference in band intensity. First, bacterial density may be lower in woolly worm tissue compared to boxelder bugs. *Wolbachia* density can vary substantially between host species and even between different tissues within the same individual [@pietri2016rich]. If woolly worms harbor lower *Wolbachia* populations, less template DNA would be available for PCR amplification, resulting in fainter bands.

Second, the efficiency of the *Wolbachia* 16S primers may differ across host-associated *Wolbachia* strains. If the *Wolbachia* strain infecting woolly worms had greater sequence divergence from the primer binding sites compared to the strain in boxelder bugs, amplification efficiency would be reduced.

Third, technical factors related to DNA extraction could contribute to the observed difference. Although both species showed strong arthropod COI bands, indicating successful DNA extraction overall, it is possible that *Wolbachia* DNA was less efficiently extracted from woolly worm tissue due to differential tissue disruption. Despite these uncertainties regarding band intensity, the detection of bands in both woolly worm samples above background levels provides evidence for *Wolbachia* presence in this species.

### Ecological Implications

The widespread presence of *Wolbachia* in both boxelder bugs and woolly worms suggests that it may influence host biology in these populations. *Wolbachia* is known to manipulate host reproduction through mechanisms such as cytoplasmic incompatibility, which reduces the reproductive success of crosses between infected and uninfected individuals, thereby promoting the spread of the bacterium through host populations (turelli2022wolbachia). If *Wolbachia* induces cytoplasmic incompatibility or other reproductive manipulations in these species, it could influence population genetics and mating dynamics.

Beyond reproductive effects, *Wolbachia* may also improve species fitness. Recent research has demonstrated that *Wolbachia* can protect insects from viral pathogens by modulating host immune responses [@teixeira2008bacterial]. If *Wolbachia* provides antiviral protection to boxelder bugs and woolly worms, infected individuals may have advantages in environments with many diseases.

### Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small, particularly for woolly worms (n = 2), limiting the precision of infection rate estimates and the statistical power to detect differences between species. The apparent 100% infection rate in woolly worms should be interpreted cautiously. Second, the PCR-based detection method provides only presence/absence data and cannot quantify how much *Wolbachia* is present.

Third, *Wolbachia* infection rates were measured at a single time point. Therefore, this study cannot address questions regarding the temporal stability of *Wolbachia* or seasonal variations in prevalence. Infection rates can fluctuate over time due to environmental factors, the host's life stage, or seasonal dynamics of host populations. A longitudinal approach with samples across multiple seasons would provide a more accurate picture of how prevalent *Wolbachia* is in these species. Finally, the detection of faint *Wolbachia* bands in woolly worms introduces some uncertainty. While bands were visible above the background, the possibility of false positives due to contamination or primer artifacts cannot be entirely excluded, though the use of separate gels for each species reduces this risk.

Despite these limitations, the results of this study provide clear evidence that *Wolbachia* is present in both boxelder bug and woolly worm populations in Burke County. The infection rates observed, particularly the 80% prevalence in boxelder bugs, are consistent with stable maternal transmission and suggest that *Wolbachia* plays an ongoing role in the biology of these local arthropod populations.

### Future Directions

First, expanding the sample size for both species would provide better estimates of infection prevalence and allow a better statistical comparison of infection rates between taxa. Sampling additional individuals from multiple locations within Burke County and from other geographic regions would also allow investigation of spatial variation in *Wolbachia* prevalence. Second, expanding the study to include additional arthropod species from the local area would provide a broader perspective on *Wolbachia* distribution across the Burke County arthropod community. Species from additional orders (such as Coleoptera, Hymenoptera, or Diptera) could be surveyed to assess whether infection rates vary systematically across taxonomic groups or primarily at the species level.

Third, functional studies investigating the effects of *Wolbachia* on host fitness, reproduction, and immunity would clarify what keeps this infection going. Experimental removal of *Wolbachia* through antibiotic treatment, followed by comparison of fitness traits between treated and control individuals, could reveal whether *Wolbachia* is helpful, harmful, or both.

Finally, given the growing interest in *Wolbachia* for biological control of disease vectors, characterizing local *Wolbachia* diversity could contribute to broader efforts to understand the evolution and ecology of this important endosymbiont.

---

## Conclusion

This study offers evidence of infection with the bacterium *Wolbachia* in two arthropod species in Burke County, North Carolina, with molecular detection revealing high infection rates in both boxelder bugs (80%, 4 out of 5 individuals) and woolly worms (100%, 2 out of 2 individuals). These results provide molecular evidence of *Wolbachia* infection, as the identities of the arthropod species were confirmed through DNA sequencing with high-quality scores, validating the infection of *Wolbachia* within *Boisea rubrolineata* and *Pyrrharctia isabella*. This confirms the presence of *Wolbachia* infection in these arthropods, which belong to taxonomically divergent groups within the local arthropod community, which is consistent with the bacterium's status as one of the most ubiquitous endosymbionts. However, many questions remain unanswered. What are the specific *Wolbachia* strains infecting these populations? Are the same strains present in both species, suggesting horizontal transmission, or do different strains infect each host? Although this study provides insight into *Wolbachia* prevalence in Southern Appalachian arthropods, future studies may help determine the ecological and evolutionary significance of these infections and their role in shaping local arthropod communities.
