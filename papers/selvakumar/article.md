title: Effects of Probiotic Yogurt Cultures from Dahi on Dextrose-Induced Type 2 Diabetic-like Symptoms in Caenorhabditis elegans
abstract: |
  Type 2 diabetes is a growing global health concern, particularly prevalent in South Asian populations. Increasing evidence suggests that the gut microbiome plays a significant role in metabolic health, and probiotic bacteria may help mitigate metabolic dysfunction. This study investigated whether lactic acid bacteria isolated from dahi (traditional Indian yogurt) could reduce impairments in *Caenorhabditis elegans* exposed to high-glucose conditions. Bacterial strains were isolated from dahi, identified through PCR amplification and BLAST sequencing as *Lactobacillus delbrueckii*, and administered to worms alongside dextrose-fed *Escherichia coli* OP50 to induce diabetic-like symptoms. Locomotion was assessed using a thrashing assay, and lipid accumulation was measured through Oil Red O staining. Worms exposed to high-glucose conditions exhibited significantly reduced thrashing rates and increased lipid accumulation compared to controls. Supplementation with *L. delbrueckii* improved locomotion at multiple time points (p < 0.05) and also reduced lipid accumulation (p = 0.00094). These findings suggest that probiotic strains isolated from dahi may mitigate glucose-induced dysfunction in *C. elegans*. This study supports the potential role of accessible fermented foods in promoting metabolic health and provides a foundation for further investigation.

## Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a growing global health problem that affects millions of people and increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, nerve damage, and other serious complications. It is caused by a mix of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors and is especially common in South Asian communities. It has been estimated that approximately 23.3% of South Asians will develop diabetes at some point in their lifetime [@patel_lessons_2025].

In recent years, research attention has increasingly focused on the gut microbiome and its role in metabolic diseases. The bacteria in the human gut can affect how the body processes sugar and fat, how much inflammation is present, and even how sensitive the body is to insulin. Probiotics, which are live microorganisms that can be beneficial to health, when consumed in adequate amounts, are one way to modulate the gut microbiome. Fermented foods such as dahi, a traditional Indian yogurt, are rich in probiotic bacteria. Because dahi is accessible and widely consumed, it has the potential to serve as an easy and low-cost way to improve metabolic health.

A model organism that is well-suited to answer these questions is *C. elegans*. This nematode is often used in biomedical research because it has a short life cycle, shares many important biological pathways with humans, and responds well to changes in diet and bacteria. Studies have shown that certain probiotic strains can influence physiological processes in *C. elegans*, including reducing fat storage, improving stress resistance, and extending lifespan [@ikeda_influence_2007; @komura_prolonged_2022].

High-glucose diets have been shown to impair locomotion, shorten lifespan, and increase susceptibility to pathogens in *C. elegans* [@kingsley_glucose-fed_2024]. Certain probiotic strains, however, may counteract these effects. For example, in mice, the Lactococcus cremoris strain isolated from Caspian Sea yogurt was shown to extend lifespan and improve locomotion [@kimoto-nira_anti-ageing_2007]. Similarly, studies using *C. elegans* as a model organism have found that *L. cremoris* can prolong lifespan, slow the rate of aging, and enhance host defense [@komura_prolonged_2022]. More broadly, lactic acid bacteria derived from yogurt have been shown to extend the lifespan of *C. elegans* when compared to worms fed only the standard laboratory diet of E. coli OP50 [@ikeda_influence_2007]. These studies indicate that probiotic bacteria have the potential to influence not just gut health, but also longevity and symptoms associated with various metabolic conditions.

Despite these findings, limited research has examined the effect of probiotic bacteria on organisms exposed to dextrose-induced diets and diabetic-like symptoms.  Therefore, this study investigates whether probiotic bacteria isolated from dahi can improve lipid metabolisation and locomotion in *C. elegans* grown in high-glucose conditions. Using a thrashing assay to assess locomotion and Oil Red O staining to quantify lipid accumulation, the hypothesis is that *L. delbrueckii*, isolated from the dahi, will reduce glucose-induced metabolic dysfunction in *C. elegans*.

## Methods

### Bacteria Extraction and Identification

To isolate lactic acid bacteria from dahi, fresh samples of dahi were obtained and subjected to serial dilution. Specifically, dahi samples were diluted six times in sterile saline solution (0.9% NaCl) to reduce bacterial density. The diluted samples were streaked onto de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar plates, a selective medium optimized for the growth of lactic acid bacteria. Plates were sealed with parafilm to maintain anaerobic-like conditions and incubated at 36 °C for 48–72 hours to allow for the development of isolated bacterial colonies.

DNA was extracted from isolated colonies using a standard bacterial DNA extraction protocol.  Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed to amplify the 16S ribosomal RNA gene. Universal bacterial primers targeting the 16S rRNA gene were used under the following thermocycler conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 minutes, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C, 30 s), and extension (72 °C, 90 s), with a final ten minute extension. Amplified PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and then sent for Sanger sequencing. 

The resulting DNA sequences were analyzed using the NCBI BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) database to identify the bacterial species through comparison with reference sequences, identifying the isolate to be *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* with a percent identity of 96.3%.

### Dextrose Concentrations and *C. elegans* Treatment

Three categories of LB broth were prepared: standard LB, LB supplemented with 0.5 g of dextrose, and LB supplemented with 0.8 g of dextrose. Each solution was filter-sterilized using a 0.22 µm syringe to remove any potential contaminants and inoculated at 36 C for 24 hours with *E. coli* OP50. Cultures were grown in a water bath and then heat-killed. 50 µL of each culture were pipetted onto nematode growth medium (NGM) plates containing 5-Fluoro-2'-deoxyuridine (FUdR) and allowed to dry for 24 hours. Fifteen *C. elegans* worms were then transferred onto each plate. Additional plates were prepared using the same method but supplemented with the isolated *L. delbrueckii* culture isolated from the dahi. 

### Thrashing Assay

Locomotion was evaluated using a thrashing assay. Individual worms were transferred to a microscope slide containing an M9 buffer. Using a microscope, the number of thrashes per minute was counted, one thrash was a full body bend by a nematode. Three worms per condition (control, 0.8% dextrose, and 1.6% dextrose) were analyzed. This was also done for the worms supplemented with *L. delbrueckii*. The average thrash count per group was calculated. This assay was repeated every three days to monitor locomotion over time.

### Oil Red O Staining Assay

Oil Red O (ORO) staining was used to measure lipid accumulation in *C. elegans*. A stock staining solution was prepared by dissolving ORO powder in isopropanol at 5 mg/mL. A working solution was then prepared by diluting 600 µL of the stock solution with 400 µL of autoclaved distilled water to achieve a 60% ORO staining solution. The prepared solution was then rotated overnight in a tube rotator and filtered prior to use. 

Worm populations were collected from NGM plates, washed using phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.01% Triton X-100 (PBST), and transferred into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Samples were centrifuged at 25 × g for 1 minute, and the supernatant was removed until approximately 0.1 mL remained, leaving a concentrated pellet. Worms were washed one to two additional times. 

Following washing, 600 µL of 60% isopropanol was added to each tube and samples were rotated for 3 minutes at room temperature to fix worms. After fixation, the ORO staining solution was added to each tube, and samples were incubated for two hours. After staining, samples were centrifuged and the supernatant was removed, leaving the stained worms. The worms were then pipetted (14 µL per sample) onto microscope slides for imaging and analysis using Fiji and Image J.

## Results

### Thrashing Assay

Worms exposed to dextrose exhibited a significant reduction in thrashing rate compared to control worms across all observed time points, indicating glucose-induced impairment of locomotion and activity (@Figure_1). Worms supplemented with *L. delbrueckii* demonstrated improved locomotion compared to worms fed dextrose alone. This improvement was statistically significant (p < 0.05) on Days 3, 5 and 10 but was not significant on Day 7.

### Oil Red O Assay

Oil Red O staining revealed significantly increased lipid accumulation in worms exposed to dextrose supplemented diets compared to control worms. Worms treated with *L. delbrueckii* alongside dextrose showed a marked reduction in lipid accumulation compared to worms exposed to dextrose-only worms. Statistical analysis confirmed this reduction was strongly significant (p = 0.00094).

## Discussion

The results of this study suggest that lactic acid bacteria, *Lactobacillus delbrueckii*, isolated from dahi exert a protective effect on *C. elegans* under high-glucose conditions. Worms exposed to these probiotic strains demonstrated significantly improved locomotions. Since reduced thrashing rate is a known marker of aging and metabolic dysfunction in *C. elegans*, these findings suggest that probiotics may mitigate glucose-induced functional decline. This significance is not completely consistent or absolute, indicating that it is still affected by various other factors. 

Additionally, Oil Red O staining revealed decreased lipid accumulation in worms fed lactic acid bacteria from dahi. Excess fat storage is an important aspect of metabolic dysfunction in *C. elegans* and has been associated with diabetes-like conditions in this model organism.The reduction in lipid accumulation observed in this study supports the hypothesis that probiotic bacteria can positively influence lipid metabolism. 

These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that lactic acid bacteria improve metabolic health in *C. elegans* and other animal models [@ikeda_influence_2007; @komura_prolonged_2022]. 

However, several limitations should be considered. The sample size for behavioral assays was relatively small, and only a single probiotic strain was tested. This study focused on locomotion and lipid accumulation but does not take into account various factors such as lifespan, glucose concentrations and metabolic pathways. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms through which probiotic bacteria can influence metabolic health. 

## Future Work

Future studies should expand on the range of assays used to evaluate metabolic health in *C. elegans.*, Direct lifespan analysis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements, and advanced glycation end product (AGE) fluorescence assays would help clarify the mechanisms.

Additionally, testing a wider range of glucose concentrations and probiotic dosages would allow for a better understanding of dose-dependent effects. Investigations of other strains of bacteria found in other similar traditional fermented foods also pose a source of significant research.
