title: Synthesis and Characterization of Carbomer-Agarose Hydrogel for Sustained Melatonin Release to Promote Traumatic Brain Injury Recovery.
abstract: | 
    The therapeutic promise of melatonin in treating traumatic brain injury (TBI) is limited by its rapid metabolic clearance, motivating the investigation of both its neuroprotective properties and strategies to extend its efficacy. In this study, melatonin’s effects on post-injury recovery are assessed alongside the evaluation of a biocompatible carbomer–agarose hydrogel as a sustained-release delivery platform. Analysis of behavioral assay data indicates that melatonin treatment significantly improves motor recovery in a *Drosophila melanogaster* model of TBI, and characterization of the hydrogel system suggests that it supports sustained melatonin release and assists in overcoming its limitations.

## Introduction & Background

### Traumatic Brain Injury

Traumatic brain injury (TBI)—damage to the central nervous system by an outside force—is a leading cause of death and disability in both children and adults. An estimated 2.5 million individuals sustain a brain injury annually, with approximately 50,000 resulting in death and 80,000 suffering permanent disability [@national_institute_of_neurological_disorders_and_stroke_traumatic_2026]. The initial trauma is referred to as the primary injury, either in the form of a penetrating (open-head) or non-penetrating (closed-head) injury. It is typically marked by focal damage, such as a fractured skull, concussions, or brain-tissue lacerations. The secondary injury of TBI—an indirect consequence of the initial trauma—is the additional damage caused by a cascade of physiological activities. Involving axonal injuries, neuroinflammatory responses, and oxidative stress, these fatal events could further compromise brain function [@common_classifications_of_tbi_2025]. This stage of TBI provides a key window for therapeutic interventions, unlike the immediate and irreversible primary injury. 

Despite decades of research, there is currently no drug therapy approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to directly repair TBI [@madhusudanan_hydrogel_2020]. Current treatments focus on supportive care to manage symptoms like pain, sleep issues, and cognitive deficiencies as opposed to addressing the underlying damage to the brain [@betourney_new_2025]. This reveals a critical gap in care, emphasizing the need to identify novel TBI therapeutics. 

### Melatonin as a Neuroprotective Agent

A particularly promising candidate is melatonin, a naturally occurring neurohormone that is widely available as an over-the-counter supplement. Though best known for regulating the circadian rhythm, it also plays a powerful role in central nervous system (CNS) repair, particularly targeting mechanisms of the secondary injury. Through melatonin receptor 1 (MT1) and melatonin receptor 2 (MT2), the Nrf2 and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways are activated in response to the secondary injury of TBI. These signaling cascades boost antioxidant defenses and apoptosis-inhibition, exerting neuroprotective effects [@xie_melatonin_2022].

Furthermore, melatonin’s fundamental characteristics as a TBI therapeutic include its ability to easily cross the blood-brain barrier, potently reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, and protect neurons from apoptotic death. More notably, external delivery of melatonin assists in the reduction of sleep disturbances—a common effect of brain injury—and the resynchronization of disrupted circadian rhythms, thus supporting recovery after TBI [@samantaray_therapeutic_2009].

However, melatonin is rapidly metabolized, cleared out by the human body within four to five hours—long before the secondary injury has fully resolved [@wu_melatonin-loaded_2025]. To address this clinical limitation, a drug delivery system where melatonin—the therapeutic agent—is released over an extended period of time could maximize its effects.      

### Hydrogel-Based Drug Delivery

Hydrogels, a form of extensively researched drug carriers, are three-dimensional polymer networks capable of absorbing large amounts of water while maintaining structural integrity. Their highly tunable nature allows for them to function as ideal drug delivery systems, especially for small, unstable molecules like melatonin. Unlike systemic delivery, hydrogels allow for localized, sustained release directly at the injury site, which is essential for brain repair. By adjusting their composition, hydrogels can even be engineered to mimic the mechanical softness of brain tissue, minimizing additional damage and improving biocompatibility, representing a timely innovation in materials chemistry research [@madhusudanan_hydrogel_2020].

To ensure compatibility with brain tissue, carbomer 974P and agarose are used in combination to form the hydrogel. Both polymers are extensively found in neurosurgical applications and biomedical research, with proven high biocompatibility [@rossi_sustained_2012]. By embedding melatonin within the hydrogel structure, the release of this powerful neurohormone could be dramatically extended, maximizing therapeutic effects. 

### Research Objectives

This study explores (1) the potential of melatonin as a neurotherapeutic agent for TBI treatment, and (2) the efficacy of a biocompatible carbomer-agarose hydrogel as a potential drug delivery system to overcome melatonin’s clinical limitations. To model TBI, a *Drosophila Melanogaster* model is used, with biological assays testing the effects of melatonin on restoring locomotor activity [@katzenberger_method_2015]. Through characterization analyses, the chemical and biomedical properties of the hydrogel system are determined [@rossi_characterization_2011].        

Ultimately, it is hypothesized that melatonin will enhance locomotor recovery in a *Drosophila melanogaster* model of TBI, and the carbomer-agarose hydrogel will effectively deliver melatonin in a sustained manner and surmount its therapeutic limitations.

## Methods

### High Impact Trauma (Hit) Device Construction

To assemble the device, the spring was first secured to a wooden board using two clamps fastened with four screws. The clamps were centered across the width of the board and positioned flush against one another, with the outer clamp aligned with the edge of the board. Prior to mounting, the clamps were bent using pliers to ensure a tight fit around the spring. When installed, the clamped end of the spring sat 1/8 inch (3.2 mm) from the board’s edge, and the free end extended 3/4 inch (19 mm) beyond it. The spring was then adjusted to lie parallel to the length of the board. The free end was wrapped once with an adhesive Velcro loop strip, with its outer edge flush with the tip of the spring.

A polyurethane pad was positioned adjacent to the wooden board at a distance such that only the bottom 1 inch of the vial could make contact with it during operation. This placement ensured that the vial remained properly aligned while preventing the spring from resting flat against the board. Both the polyurethane pad and the wooden board were secured firmly in place using C-clamps to prevent any movement during testing. A digital angle gauge was aligned alongside the length of the board so that a reading of 90° corresponded to the spring in its fully vertical, pulled-back position.

### Hit Device Operation & Calibration

Between 10 and 60 young flies (0–3 days old) were placed into an empty vial and secured with a tight-fitting cotton ball. The flies were confined to the bottom 1 inch (2.5 cm) of the vial by pushing the cotton ball downward to the 1-inch mark, which was drawn on the vial to ensure consistency. After loading, the flies were allowed to recover mobility from CO₂ anesthesia for 5 minutes. The end of the spring was then inserted into the vial until the inner edge of the Velcro strip was flush with the top of the vial. When the spring rested in its horizontal position, approximately 1 inch (2.5 cm) of the vial overlapped with the raised region of the polyurethane pad.

During operation, the board and polyurethane pad were secured to the benchtop using C-clamps. The spring was pulled straight back to a 90° angle using a digital angle gauge for precise alignment, then released to deliver the strike. After the spring came to a complete stop, the flies were allowed to recover for 30 seconds. Each group received four total strikes, with a 30-second rest period between impacts. Following the final strike, the flies were given a 10-minute recovery period before being transferred to a fresh food vial. Untreated control flies were handled identically but without spring deflection or impact.

To calibrate the HIT device for mild TBI, the mortality index at 24 hours post-injury (MI$_{24}$) was measured—an MI$_{24}$ of 19.0 ± 4.0 was expected. Prior to injury, the number of flies used for each trial was recorded, and 10–60 CO₂-anesthetized w1118 flies were transferred into a vial. Twenty-four hours after injury, the number of dead flies in each trial was counted, and the MI24 was calculated as the percentage of flies that died using the formula:

$\mathrm{MI}_{24} = \frac{\text{Number of dead flies}}{\text{Total number of flies}} \times 100$

### Melatonin Treatement & Rapid Iterative Negative Geotaxis (Ring) Assay

A 4 mM melatonin treatment was prepared by first dissolving 13.94 mg of melatonin in a mixture of 1.50 mL ethanol and 14.85 mL normal saline (0.9% NaCl), which was then incorporated into standard *Drosophila* medium. Flies received this melatonin treatment for 5 days, with the melatonin-containing medium refreshed every other day to account for compound degradation. 

The study included four experimental groups: uninjured flies, uninjured flies receiving melatonin, injured flies, and injured flies receiving melatonin. To conduct the RING assay, flies were transferred into empty 10-cm vials marked with 1-cm intervals for each trial.

The recording apparatus was set up, and the vial was tapped against the table five times. Fly climbing activity was then recorded for 15 seconds, and this procedure was repeated nine times per group. The number of flies that crossed the 4-cm mark within 10 seconds was counted to evaluate locomotor performance.

### Hydrogel Synthesis & Melatonin Loading

A 100 mL aliquot of PBS (pH 7.4) was measured into a 250 mL beaker, equipped with a magnetic stir bar, and stirred at 300–500 rpm on a hot plate. Agarose (1.20 g) and Carbomer 974P (0.30 g) were weighed using an analytical balance and slowly added to the vortex of the stirring PBS over 1–2 minutes. The mixture was stirred for an additional 10 minutes to obtain a uniform, cloudy dispersion free of clumps or sediment. 

While stirring, 1 M NaOH was added dropwise and the pH was monitored every 30 seconds; titration was stopped once the pH reached 7.0 ± 0.1. A melatonin stock solution (1 mg/mL) was prepared separately by dissolving melatonin in a mixture of absolute ethanol and PBS and protected from light using foil.   

Following preparation of the polymer mixture, 9 mL of the hydrogel solution was transferred to a flask and heated in a microwave (500 W) in three increments (30 s/30 s/10 s) for a total of 1 minute and 10 seconds, swirling between each heating step. The hydrogel was heated uncovered until a homogeneous solution was obtained. The mixture was then allowed to cool to 40–45 °C, monitored using a temperature probe. 

Once at the target temperature, 1 mL of the melatonin stock solution was added per 9 mL of hydrogel using a micropipette, and the solution was gently stirred to ensure uniform distribution while minimizing air bubble formation. The melatonin-loaded hydrogel was immediately cast into 96-well plates to form uniform hydrogel disks and allowed to gel at 4 °C. 

After gelation, the hydrogels were trimmed to approximately 5 mm in diameter and 5 mm in thickness. The final hydrogel disks were stored at 4 °C and protected from light with foil wrap until use.

### Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) Spectroscopy

Melatonin-loaded hydrogels were transferred to a rotary evaporator and dried for 24 hours, or until a constant weight was achieved, under a reduced pressure of 10 mbar. 

Each dry hydrogel sample was ground into a fine powder using a clean, dry mortar and pestle. Approximately 1–2 mg of the powdered hydrogel was thoroughly mixed with 100 mg of spectroscopic-grade potassium bromide (KBr), and the mixture was pressed into a thin, transparent pellet using a hydraulic pellet press under vacuum. 

Transmission FTIR spectroscopy was performed on the prepared KBr pellets, ensuring proper instrument calibration. Spectra were collected from hydrogels immediately following synthesis, gelation, and drying.

### Melatonin-Release Analysis

Prior to release testing, melatonin-loaded hydrogels were allowed to swell to equilibrium by submerging them overnight at 4 °C in excess melatonin solution (1 mg/mL). After swelling, each hydrogel was transferred into an individual well of a clean 24-well plate, with surfaces gently blotted using moistened filter paper to remove loosely bound buffer prior to transfer. 1 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) was added to each well to fully submerge the hydrogels, and the plates were wrapped with tin foil for protection against light before incubation at 37 °C for the duration of the release study.                                        

Melatonin standard solutions were prepared to enable quantification. A series of standards (1, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 µg/mL) was generated by diluting a 1 mg/mL melatonin stock solution with 10% ethanol in PBS to maintain consistent solvent composition across measurements. Absorbance was measured at 278 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, following blanking with 10% ethanol in PBS. Standard absorbance values were plotted against concentration, and a linear regression equation (y = mx + b) was obtained to construct the calibration curve used for subsequent sample calculations.

At predetermined time points (Days 1, 2, 3, and 7), each well was gently mixed by pipetting three times with a P1000 pipette prior to sampling. A 100 µL aliquot of supernatant was collected from each well and immediately replaced with 100 µL of fresh PBS to maintain constant volume and sink conditions. The collected aliquots were transferred to quartz cuvettes and diluted with 2.9 mL of 10% ethanol in PBS, followed by gentle mixing prior to analysis. Absorbance at 278 nm was measured after blanking the instrument with 10% ethanol in PBS, and melatonin concentrations were calculated using the previously generated standard curve.

### Swelling Behavior Analysis

Melatonin-loaded hydrogels were transferred to a rotary evaporator and dried for 24 hours, or until a constant weight was achieved, under a reduced pressure of 10 mbar. The dried hydrogels were weighed using an analytical balance, and the initial dry mass was recorded as W$_d$(0). The samples were then submerged in excess PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours to allow complete swelling.

To evaluate swelling behavior under varying pH conditions, each swollen hydrogel was transferred into an individual well of a clean 24-well plate. 2.0 mL of PBS adjusted to either pH 1.0, 7.4, or 13.0 was added to each well to fully submerge the hydrogels, and the plates were wrapped with tin foil for protection against light before incubation at 37 °C for the duration of the release study.                                          

At days 1, 2, 3, and 7, hydrogels were removed from their respective buffers, gently blotted to remove loosely bound solvent, and weighed to record W$_d$(t) for each pH condition. The swelling ratio was calculated using the equation:

$\mathrm{Swelling\ Ratio\ (\%)} = \frac{W_{\text{w}}(t) - W_{\text{d}}(0)}{W_{\text{d}}(0)} \times 100$,

where W$_w$(t) is the wet hydrogel mass after swelling and W$_d$(0) is the initial dry hydrogel mass.

### Mass Degradation Analysis

Melatonin-loaded hydrogels were transferred to a rotary evaporator and dried for 24 hours, or until a constant weight was achieved, under a reduced pressure of 10 mbar. The dried hydrogels were weighed using an analytical balance, and the initial dry mass was recorded as W$_d$(0). Following drying, the hydrogels were submerged in excess PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C to allow controlled swelling and degradation. Samples were maintained for 1, 2, 3, and 7 days, with the buffer replaced daily to ensure consistent degradation conditions. At each designated time point, hydrogels were removed from the buffer, gently blotted using moistened filter paper to remove loosely bound solvent, and dried once again overnight using the rotary evaporator at 10 mbar until completely dry. The dry mass of each sample was then recorded. Mass loss over time was quantified using the degradation ratio formula:

$\mathrm{Mass\ Degradation\ (\%)} = \frac{W_{\text{d}}(t) - W_{\text{d}}(0)}{W_{\text{d}}(0)} \times 100$,

where W$_d$(t) represents the dry mass at time t and W$_d$(0) is the initial dry mass.

## Results

### Rapid Iterative Negative Geotaxis (Ring) Assay

RING Assay Results

Four experimental groups were analyzed: uninjured flies (No Injury), uninjured flies receiving melatonin (No Injury + Melatonin), injured flies (Injury), and injured flies receiving melatonin (Injury + Melatonin). As shown in the figure, there is no significant difference in motor performance between the No Injury and No Injury + Melatonin groups, indicating that melatonin alone did not affect baseline mobility. In contrast, the Injury group exhibits a significant reduction in motor ability compared with the No Injury group (P < 0.0001), which demonstrates that the induced injury produced a measurable impairment in motor function. Notably, melatonin treatment after injury led to a substantial improvement in motor recovery, with the Injury + Melatonin group performing significantly better than the Injury group (P < 0.0001). Additionally, motor performance in the Injury + Melatonin group is not significantly different from that of the No Injury group, suggesting near-complete recovery. Interestingly, flies in the Injury + Melatonin group also regained the ability to jump, a behavior not observed in the untreated Injury group.

### Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) Spectroscopy

```{figure} images/qu_fig_2.png
:name: Figure_2
:align: center

FTIR Spectrum of Melatonin-Loaded Carbomer-Agarose Hydrogel
```

FTIR spectroscopy confirms the presence of characteristic functional groups from the carbomer-agarose hydrogel and verifies the successful incorporation of melatonin. The spectrum exhibits a broad band between 3300–3500 cm$^{-1}$, attributed to O–H stretching from the carbomer, agarose, and bound water within the hydrogel; N-H stretching from melatonin also contributes in this region. 
 
A distinct peak at approximately 1700 cm$^{-1}$ corresponds to the C=O stretching vibration of carbomer’s carboxylic acid groups, consistent with the polymer’s expected structure. Several additional bands within the 1500–1650 cm$^{-1}$ region were observed, which include contributions from melatonin’s aromatic ring vibrations.

The fingerprint region contained multiple peaks between 1000–1200 cm$^{-1}$, representing C–O and C–O–C stretching vibrations from agarose’s glycosidic linkages. Additional features between 700–900 cm$^{-1}$ correspond to out-of-plane aromatic bending modes from melatonin, supporting its incorporation into the hydrogel matrix.

Overall, the FTIR spectrum demonstrates retention of the hydrogel’s polysaccharide and carboxylate functional groups and verified the presence of melatonin through its characteristic aromatic vibrational peaks.

### Melatonin Release Analysis

```{figure} images/qu_fig_3.png
:name: Figure_3
:align: center

Melatonin Percent Release From Carbomer-Agarose Hydrogels
```

Melatonin release studies show a reproducible, sustained diffusion pattern across the hydrogel samples. During the first 24 hours, each hydrogel exhibits an initial burst phase, releasing approximately 35–45% of the loaded melatonin. This continued in a time-dependent manner through Day 3, at which point samples reached 55–70% cumulative release. By Day 7, all the hydrogel samples converged to a final release of 80–90%, indicating that most encapsulated melatonin had diffused out of the matrix. The gradual increase of melatonin over time reflects controlled release by hydrogel swelling and polymer mesh degradation.

### Swelling Behavior Analysis

```{figure} images/qu_fig_4.png
:name: Figure_4
:align: center

Swelling Ratios of Carbomer-Agarose Hydrogels Across Various pH Levels
```

Swelling behavior analysis reveals distinct pH-dependent patterns over the 7-day period. At pH 1.0, the hydrogels exhibit the lowest swelling, reaching a maximum swelling ratio of approximately 400% by Day 1 and remaining relatively constant through Day 7. In contrast, hydrogels submerged at physiological pH 7.4 demonstrate significantly greater buffer uptake, with swelling increasing rapidly to roughly 1000% by Day 1 and gradually rising to approximately 1200% by Day 7. Hydrogels exposed to alkaline pH 13.0 display an intermediate profile: swelling reached roughly 700% at Day 1, followed by a steady, moderate increase to approximately 850% at Day 7. Across all conditions, most expansion occurs within the first 24 hours, after which swelling progresses more slowly. These results indicate that hydrogel network expansion is strongly influenced by environmental pH, with maximum swelling occurring near physiological conditions.

### Mass Degradation Analysis

```{figure} images/qu_fig_5.png
:name: Figure_5
:align: center

Mass Degradation Ratios of Carbomer-Agarose Hydrogels
```

Mass degradation studies demonstrate a time-dependent loss of hydrogel mass during incubation in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 ℃. The hydrogel samples display an initial rapid degradation phase, losing approximately 47% of their mass within the first 24 hours. Degradation increased to 58% at both days 2 and 3, indicating a sustained but temporarily plateauing erosion of the polymer network. By day 7, mass loss reached 64%. 

This progressive decrease in mass is consistent with hydrolytic cleavage occurring within the carbomer-agarose matrix. The polymeric framework can be approximated as a carbon-based (C–C) backbone derived from carbomer 974P, ester-linked to agarose and partially carboxylated by the ionic salts present in PBS. As esters undergo hydrolysis, they convert into carboxylic acids and alcohol groups, which weakens cross-linking points and promotes gradual disintegration of the network, undergoing the proposed reaction: 

$\mathrm{RCO_2R'} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{RCO_2H} + \mathrm{R'OH}$

The progressive mass loss at each time point indicates ongoing hydrolytic breakdown of the hydrogel matrix. Overall, the carbomer–agarose hydrogel experiences substantial degradation during the first three days, followed by a slower but persistent loss of mass.

## Discussion

The results of this study highlight both the neuroprotective potential of melatonin in a *Drosophila* model of TBI and the viability of a carbomer-agarose hydrogel as a simple, biocompatible sustained-release delivery platform. While the overall data support the value of this combined therapeutic approach, several limitations must be considered when interpreting and extrapolating these findings, as they influence the validity and reproducibility of the conclusions drawn. 

## TBI Induction Variability & Ring Assay Limitations

A limitation of this study was the inconsistency of TBI induction using the HIT device. Although the device was calibrated to produce a mild injury characterized by an MI$_{24}$ index of approximately 19%, only a small subset of injury attempts produced mortality levels within the expected range—indicating the variability that accompanies HIT device construction. This lack of reproducibility likely stems from subtle differences in vial alignment or spring design and release mechanics, all of which can markedly influence injury severity. Such inconsistency introduces uncertainty into whether all injury groups experienced equivalent levels of TBI, complicating interpretation of recovery. 

Additionally, the small sample size of flies used in the RING assays likely contributed to the size of the Injury + Melatonin group’s error bars. With only seven flies in each control and experimental group, the impact of outliers may be more pronounced, potentially skewing the results and increasing the variability of the data. Additionally, the 4 mM melatonin treatment was administered for only five days rather than the originally planned seven days due to time constraints. This reduces the flies’ duration of therapeutic exposure, potentially underestimating melatonin’s full neuroprotective effects. 

These limitations notwithstanding, the observed trends are promising and suggest that further investigation with larger sample sizes and extended treatment periods could strengthen the findings and clarify the therapeutic promise of melatonin for TBI treatment.

### Melatonin Improves Locomotor Function After Injury

Nonetheless, the behavioral data indicate that melatonin treatment enhanced locomotor performance in injured flies, with treated individuals approaching the motor ability of uninjured controls. The restoration of jumping behavior—absent in untreated injured flies—provides further evidence of functional recovery. Additionally, the improvement in climbing performance as assessed by the RING assay suggests that melatonin also supports recovery of endurance following injury. Importantly, these improvements were observed even with a shortened treatment period of five days, suggesting that melatonin’s beneficial effects may emerge relatively quickly. Although the small sample sizes warrant cautious interpretation, the clear differences between groups strongly suggest that melatonin supported recovery under the conditions tested.

### Constraints in Hydrogel Characterization

The hydrogel characterization component of the study similarly faced limitations. Melatonin-release testing was conducted with four hydrogel samples; thus, increasing the sample size would more accurately capture the variability or consistency in polymer structure or drug-loading efficiency. 

The pH-dependent swelling behavior analyses reveal asymmetry in sample distribution: while physiological pH 7.4 included eight hydrogels, the extreme pH conditions (pH 1.0 and pH 13.0) were examined with three hydrogels per group. These particular conditions were selected to represent environments relevant to acute post-injury shifts in tissue acidity and alkalinity, with the hydrogels displaying distinct and chemically consistent swelling patterns across each environment.

Despite the limitations, the hydrogels behaved mechanically and chemically as expected. FTIR spectroscopy confirmed the successful incorporation of melatonin into the hydrogel network, and both swelling and degradation studies showed physiologically reasonable trends, reinforcing the material’s potential for biomedical use.

### Significance & Strengths

Although the study is limited by the methodological and sample-size limitations outlined above, it also details several strengths that support the relevance and promise of the work. The integration of a biological TBI model with a materials-engineered drug delivery system represents a novel approach that bridges behavioral neuroscience with polymer chemistry. The hydrogel formulation developed here is simple, reproducible, and composed of biocompatible materials commonly used in medical and research settings. Its sustained-release aligns well with the therapeutic time window during which secondary-injury processes unfold in TBI, supporting its potential relevance for future translational applications.

Overall, the study provides compelling preliminary evidence that melatonin can improve functional recovery after TBI and that a carbomer–agarose hydrogel offers a promising system for sustained, physiologically relevant melatonin delivery. The results lay a strong foundation for future, more comprehensive investigations.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the therapeutic promise of melatonin for promoting recovery from TBI and introduces a novel approach to overcoming its rapid clearance through a carbomer-agarose hydrogel delivery system. The results show that melatonin treatment significantly improves motor function in a *Drosophila* model of TBI, with treated flies recovering nearly full locomotor capacity, including climbing, jumping, and endurance—activities that are typically impaired after injury. These findings reinforce melatonin’s neuroprotective effects and its promise as an effective therapeutic during the post-injury recovery phase.

The carbomer-agarose hydrogel, engineered for sustained-release delivery, successfully encapsulated melatonin and released it in a controlled, time-dependent manner, addressing the challenge of its rapid metabolism and clearance. The pH-responsive swelling behavior and gradual degradation of the hydrogel further demonstrate its suitability for dynamic physiological environments, establishing it as an ideal candidate for localized drug delivery to the injury site. This capability could significantly improve the effectiveness of melatonin treatment by extending its presence at the brain injury location, thereby enhancing its neuroprotective effects over time.

While there are some limitations to this study, the data provide strong evidence for the combined potential of melatonin and hydrogel-based delivery systems in TBI treatment. Together, these findings suggest that melatonin, in conjunction with the hydrogel delivery system, holds considerable promise for advancing the treatment of brain injuries.

### Future Work

While this study provides promising insights into the potential of melatonin-loaded carbomer–agarose hydrogels for TBI treatment, several critical questions must be addressed in future research. For instance, how does the extended duration of melatonin’s neuroprotective effects, facilitated by the hydrogel, influence key cellular pathways, such as oxidative stress reduction, neuroinflammation modulation, and neuronal survival? Understanding these interactions on a molecular level will be essential for optimizing the therapeutic approach.

Additionally, although melatonin alone demonstrated promise in improving motor recovery, the synergistic effects of combining melatonin with other neuroprotective agents in the hydrogel system are yet to be explored. Could the inclusion of additional compounds—such as antioxidants, growth factors, or anti-inflammatory agents—enhance the therapeutic efficacy of the hydrogel? Furthermore, is there a dose-response relationship with melatonin administration, and if so, what is the optimal dosage and release profile to maximize neuroprotection without inducing side effects?

Another critical area for future investigation is the precise impact of the hydrogel's degradation rate on melatonin release kinetics. While this study demonstrated the hydrogel’s capacity for sustained release, the current degradation profile may need refinement to achieve a more controlled release over the therapeutic window. What is the ideal degradation rate to align with the temporal dynamics of secondary injury processes in TBI? Fine-tuning the hydrogel’s degradation behavior could significantly enhance its clinical applicability and effectiveness in long-term recovery.

The potential for this system to be used not only as a treatment for TBI but also for preventing neurodegeneration in other conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease or stroke, warrants exploration as well. Could this hydrogel system be adapted for prophylactic use in individuals at risk of neurological injury or neurodegenerative diseases? Moreover, how can the hydrogel be tailored to deliver different agents based on specific pathological features, such as pH or enzyme activity, to provide more personalized treatment options?

While *Drosophila* models provided valuable insights into melatonin's efficacy, future studies should prioritize translating these findings into more complex vertebrate models, such as rodents. These models more closely replicate human TBI pathology and will allow for better evaluation of the pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and long-term effects of the melatonin-loaded hydrogel.

Furthermore, research into effective delivery methods for the hydrogel in vertebrate models is needed—whether through direct injection to the injury site, surgical implantation, or other minimally invasive approaches—to ensure it can be practically and safely administered in clinical settings.

Ultimately, by extending melatonin's neuroprotective effects, this approach could reduce oxidative stress, modulate inflammation, and support neuronal survival, potentially enhancing recovery and improving functional outcomes for those suffering from TBI. Such innovations are crucial in moving beyond symptom management and toward therapies that truly repair the brain’s injury, providing hope for more effective, long-lasting treatments in the future.
