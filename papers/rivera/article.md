## Introduction

Huntington's disease (HD) is a currently incurable, fatal neurodegenerative disorder that impairs voluntary movement, cognition, and behavior. It occurs in 0.005–0.01% of individuals in the Caucasian population and is characterized by choreiform movements, bradykinesia and rigidity, cognitive decline, depression, and other psychiatric symptoms [@saudou_biology_2016]. 

HD is caused by a mutation in the *HTT* gene, which encodes the huntingtin (Htt) protein. In healthy individuals, the *HTT* gene contains 15 to 35 consecutive repetitions of the CAG (cytosine-adenine-guanine) codon. These CAG repeats translate into a polyglutamine (polyQ) domain—a long sequence of the amino acid glutamine. In HD, this chain exceeds 36 glutamines [@myers_huntingtons_2004]. The length of the polyQ chain is inversely proportional to the disease age of onset—40-50 repeats lead to adult-onset HD, while 50-120 repeats lead to a juvenile form of the disease [@squitieri_cag_2002]. The juvenile form, however, is rare and occurs in only 5% of cases [@saudou_biology_2016]. Within brain cells, affected Htt misfolds and forms aggregates with toxic properties [@hatters_protein_2008]. These aggregates disrupt normal cellular processes, leading to neurodegeneration and the aforementioned symptoms from HD.

A feature of HD pathology is the disruption of glucose metabolism in the brain, including significantly reduced glucose uptake in key regions such as the striatum and prefrontal cortex [@cepeda-prado_r6/2_2012]. These metabolic deficits contribute to the energy depletion and progressive neurodegeneration characteristic of the disease. Emerging evidence has linked HD to the insulin signaling pathway, which plays a key role in regulating glucose metabolism. An in vitro study demonstrated that activating Insulin Receptor Substrate 2 (IRS2) reduced huntingtin protein aggregates and increased autophagy [@yamamoto_autophagy-mediated_2006]. Building on this, an *in vivo* *Drosophila melanogaster* model of polyglutamine disease showed that glipizide—a drug that promotes insulin secretion—reduced neurodegeneration and improved motor function [@tandon_glipizide_2023]. Together, these findings suggest that supporting glucose metabolism and insulin signaling may offer a therapeutic avenue for HD.

Fig leaves have been used in traditional Latin American medicine for centuries and may be able to provide this support naturally. Studies have shown that fig leaf tea may lower blood glucose levels in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), suggesting a capacity to influence glucose uptake and utilization [@ganika_br_bangun_effect_2021]. 

This study explored the therapeutic potential of FLE to mitigate HD symptoms using a *Drosophila melanogaster* model. It was hypothesized that flies receiving FLE would exhibit improved motor function and increased lifespan, consistent with a partial restoration of metabolic function in the HD model.

## Methods

1. Plant-based material and extract preparation

Leaves were collected from a *Ficus carica* tree in Morganton, NC. The fig leaves were dried at 40 ºC. After drying, the leaves were powdered and extracted using 45% ethanol at a ratio of 1:15 (w/v) using vacuum filtration. The resulting liquid extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 50ºC to yield FLE. The crude extract was stored between 2–8ºC until further use.

2. *Drosophila* rearing and extract administration

All *Drosophila* lines used in this study were procured from Bloomington *Drosophila* Stock Center, Indiana, USA. Virgin females from UAS-Httex1pQ93 (mutant HD model) strains were crossed with males carrying the elav-GAL4 pan-neuronal driver. The resulting female progeny—elav-GAL4>UAS-Httex1p Q93 (HD model) were used for all experimental assays. Flies were kept at approximately 28ºC and 70% humidity and fed a standard diet (Carolina Biological Instant Drosophila Medium). Experimental groups were fed diets supplemented with FLE at final concentrations of 8 and 16 mg/mL. The dose range is based on previous studies using plant-based extracts in *Drosophila* [@raj_discriminatory_2020].

3. Feeding assay

To ensure that changes in health or motor ability were not due to altered food intake, a dye-ingestion assay was performed as previously described [@raj_discriminatory_2020]. UAS-HTT flies were not provided food for approximately 24 hours to standardize hunger levels. They were allowed to feed for 30 minutes at approximately 20ºC on food mixed with 0.5% blue dye (FD & C Blue Dye No. 1). After feeding, flies were anesthetized, and the midgut of the alimentary canal was removed and homogenized in PBS. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,500 r/min for 10 minutes. Absorbance was recorded at 625 nm using a spectrophotometer. Absorbance values were normalized to the total weight of flies per replicate. Two replicates per treatment condition were performed for this assay, and each treatment condition had 5 female flies.

4. Survival assay

To evaluate the effect of FLE on viability, newly eclosed flies from *UAS-HTT x Elav-Gal4* (HD model) were transferred to vials containing standard *Drosophila* medium supplemented with FLE at concentrations of 0, 8, and 16 mg/mL. Two replicates per treatment condition were performed for this assay. The total number of deaths was recorded after 19–20 days. The ratio between living adults to the total number of flies originally transferred was used to represent the survival rate, for each genotype and treatment condition.

5. Negative geotaxis assay

Motor coordination and neuromuscular function was assessed using a negative geotaxis assay. From each treatment group, 15 adult flies were placed into vertically-oriented transparent plastic tubes, marked for height calibration. The tube was gently tapped against the table five times to bring all the flies to the bottom. Immediately afterward, the tube was placed upright, and the flies' climbing behavior was recorded for 10 seconds with a camera positioned in front of the tube. The amount of flies to make it past the 7 cm mark were noted. The average score for each group was calculated and compared across treatments to determine the effect of fig leaf extract on locomotor impairment in Huntington's disease model flies. Ten trials were completed for this assay.	

## Results

1. Feeding assay

The feeding assay revealed no statistically significant differences in food intake across FLE treatment groups (@Figure_1). Normalized absorbance values were comparable among HD model flies administered 0, 8, and 16 mg/mL FLE, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA. These findings indicate that the observed differences in other assays cannot be attributed to alterations in feeding behavior.

2. Longevity assay

Survival rates assessed 19–20 days post-eclosure differed significantly across treatment groups (@Figure_2). HD model flies administered 8 mg/mL of FLE exhibited significantly greater survival compared to both the untreated control group (0 mg/mL) and flies administered 16 mg/mL of FLE, as determined by one-way ANOVA. No statistically significant difference in survival was observed between the untreated control and the 16 mg/mL treatment group. These results suggest that FLE at 8 mg/mL has a survival benefit in this HD model, while the higher dose does not show the same effect.

3. Negative geotaxis (climbing) assay

Motor performance assessed 15 days post-eclosure differed significantly across treatment groups (@Figure_3). HD model flies administered 8 mg/mL of FLE demonstrated significantly greater climbing performance, as measured by the amount of flies surpassing the 7 cm threshold within 10 seconds, compared to both the untreated control and the 16 mg/mL treatment group (p<0.0001). No statistically significant difference in climbing performance was observed between the untreated control and the 16 mg/mL treatment group. These results indicate that FLE at 8 mg/mL ameliorates locomotor impairment in HD model flies, whereas the 16 mg/mL dose did not generate any improvement.

## Discussion

The results of this study provide preliminary evidence that FLE may alleviate symptoms of Huntington's disease (HD) in a *Drosophila melanogaster* model. Across two key assays—longevity and motor function—HD model flies administered 8 mg/mL FLE consistently outperformed both untreated controls and flies receiving the higher 16 mg/mL dose. The feeding assay confirmed that these differences cannot be attributed to FLE-induced changes in appetite, strengthening the possibility that FLE exerts a direct neuroprotective or metabolic effect.

The survival and motor improvements observed at 8 mg/mL are consistent with the broader hypothesis that supporting glucose metabolism may partially offset HD-related neurodegeneration. HD is characterized by significantly reduced glucose uptake in the brain, contributing to the energy deficits that drive progressive neuronal loss [@cepeda-prado_r6/2_2012]. Fig leaves are associated with modulation of glucose uptake and utilization, as evidenced by studies showing that fig leaf tea reduces blood glucose levels in patients with type 2 diabetes [@ganika_br_bangun_effect_2021]. It is therefore plausible that one or more of the bioactive compounds within the leaves acts to support cellular energy metabolism in HD-affected neurons, partially compensating for the impaired glucose uptake that underlies neurodegeneration. However, the specific mechanism by which FLE may interact with glucose metabolism in this model is still undetermined.

A notable finding is the dose-response pattern: the 16 mg/mL treatment group did not differ significantly from the untreated control in either survival or climbing performance, despite the 8 mg/mL group showing significant improvements in both. This non-linear relationship suggests the existence of a therapeutic window for FLE, above which beneficial effects may be lost. High concentrations of this extract may interfere with normal metabolic processes or have toxins that offset its benefits.

## Limitations

It is also important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, it lacks a wild-type (non-HD) control group, making it difficult to be certain whether the observed benefits are specific to HD pathology or reflect general beneficial health enhancements of FLE. Second, the replication levels varied across assays. The climbing assay, with 10 replicates per condition, is reasonably well-powered and its findings carry greater statistical confidence. The feeding and survival assays, however, were each conducted with only two replicates per condition, which limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn from those results specifically.


## Conclusion

This study set out to investigate whether *Ficus carica* leaf extract could reduce the severity of Huntington's disease symptoms in a *Drosophila melanogaster* model, with the hypothesis that FLE would improve motor function and increase longevity by supporting glucose metabolism. The results support the hypothesis that HD model flies administered 8 mg/mL FLE showed significantly greater survival and significantly better climbing performance compared to untreated controls. These findings suggest that FLE has therapeutic potential as a natural, plant-based treatment for HD, likely through support of metabolic processes disrupted by the disease. However, these conclusions are preliminary and should be treated as a foundation for further investigation rather than definitive evidence.


### Future Work

Several experiments could be performed to build on and refine these findings. First, testing a broader range of FLE concentrations would help characterize the dose-response relationship more precisely and better define the therapeutic window. Second, including wild-type *Drosophila* as a control in all future experiments would allow researchers to distinguish HD-specific effects from general effects of the extract on fly health and longevity.

To directly test the proposed mechanism, future studies should measure markers of glucose metabolism in FLE-treated and untreated HD flies to determine whether FLE produces a measurable improvement in cellular energy availability. Analysis of photoreceptor degeneration using a pseudopupil assay would also provide a valuable insight regarding neurodegeneration.

Additionally, identifying which specific bioactive compounds in FLE are responsible for the observed effects would be important toward understanding the mechanism of action. Increasing replication in the feeding and survival assays would increase reliability and consistency of data across all measures.
