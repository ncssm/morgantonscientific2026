title: Evaluating the Attenuating Effects of LAB-Fermented Black Garlic (Allium sativum) in the Drosophila melanogaster Model of Inflammatory Bowel Disease by ROS Scavenging
abstract: | 
    Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is a chronic immune disease in which emerging evidence suggests that excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) promote lipid peroxidation, contributing to intestinal epithelial damage and increased gut permeability. As a potential antioxidant intervention, homemade black garlic was produced from the heat-induced aging of raw garlic, and extracts were prepared with and without lactic acid bacteria (LAB) fermentation. Antioxidant activity was assessed *in vitro* using a standard DPPH free radical scavenging assay. To model IBD-like intestinal damage, *Drosophila melanogaster* were administered 7% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) in a 5% sucrose solution using a filter paper feeding setup. Experimental groups received increasing concentrations of fermented and non-fermented extract through the same feeding procedure. The purpose of the existing study was to evaluate whether black garlic extract can attenuate DSS-induced gut permeability *in vivo* and whether LAB fermentation alters this effect. The attenuation of gut permeability was visually assessed using a Smurf assay. Permeability was quantified in ImageJ by calculating the fraction of blue-stained gut area relative to total gut area, with results indicating a dose-dependent reduction in dye leakage and similar trends across both fermented and non-fermented extracts. LAB fermentation may confer non-antioxidant-related benefits through mechanisms beyond direct ROS scavenging. Regardless, black garlic emerges as a promising candidate for accessible food-based treatments for attenuating gut permeability in IBD.

## Introduction

### Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is characterized by chronic inflammation in the gastrointestinal tract. Its two main forms are Crohn’s disease and Ulcerative Colitis, where victims frequently suffer from intermittent abdominal pain, chronic diarrhea, and the passage of blood in their stool. Emerging as a global public health concern, future IBD incidence cases are projected to rise from ~3.8 million in 2021 to ~4.2-5.1 million by 2050, threatening to continually burden healthcare operations [@yang_west_2025].

Although the underlying cause of IBD remains elusive, scientists hypothesize that genetic susceptibility, disturbed microflora, environmental influences, and an abnormal immune response appear to be causal factors [@keshav_dextran_2022]. The intestinal barrier is a physical barrier composed of a monolayer of epithelial cells closely bound by proteins known as tight junctions, which protects the underlying tissue from the contents of the intestinal lumen. Disruption of the intestinal barrier in IBD permits the infiltration of microbial products through the lamina propria, a connective tissue layer beneath the intestinal epithelium. These microbial products are recognized by innate immune cells through pattern recognition receptors, triggering inflammation and the recruitment of additional cells. [@saez_pathophysiology_2023]. Activated immune cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) as part of the host’s physiological response, which can contribute to collateral tissue damage when produced excessively. 

In IBD, collateral tissue damage originates from oxidative stress, a state where ROS are produced at abnormally high levels, and oxidizes polyunsaturated fatty acids in the intestinal epithelial lining via lipid peroxidation. A key byproduct of this process is the formation of malondialdehyde (MDA), which are highly reactive and cause cellular damage and inflammation. MDA, a lipid peroxide, has been found at elevated levels in the intestinal tissue, blood, and saliva of IBD patients. Notably, such elevations correlate with increased disease severity, highlighting MDA as both a reliable biomarker of oxidative stress and a potential indicator of disease progression [@muro_emerging_2024]. Therefore, the use of antioxidant compounds may alleviate the severity of intestinal damage by scavenging ROS and preventing downstream damage from lipid peroxidation.

### Black Garlic

Black garlic is produced by aging raw white garlic at high temperatures (40-90ºC) under high humidity (60-90%) for a relatively long period of time, ranging from 10-90 days. The aging and heating process converts raw garlic’s unstable, pungent compounds into water-soluble antioxidants such as S-allylcysteine (SAC) and polyphenols, among other products, thus resulting in more functional compounds than raw white garlic [@paulauskiene_changes_2024]. Notably, emerging research indicates that lactic acid bacteria (LAB) fermentation of black garlic confers an enhancement in antioxidant activity *in vitro* through its DPPH free radical scavenging activity. Fermentation is the metabolism carried out by microorganisms, which yields enhanced properties. Specifically, fermentation involving three strains of LAB, *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* X7021, X7022, and *Limosilactobacillus fermentum* S1L23, led to a 3.2 to 4.5-fold increase in SAC compared to non-fermented black garlic [@ro_improvement_2022]. Previous studies have administered black garlic to *Drosophila* in age-related toxicity assays, in which there was no significant toxicity detected at concentrations up to 4 mg/mL [@toledano_medina_physicochemical_2019]. However, testing its efficacy in an *in vivo* gut inflammation model has yet to be elucidated. Hence, this existing study seeks to explore how fermentation-derived bioactive compounds can assist in the alleviation of lipid peroxidation in the context of IBD. 

### *Drosophila* as a Model Organism

Due to the high conservation in gut structure and function with mammals, in addition to its ability to serve as a tractable model for human inflammatory responses, including intestinal barrier dysfunction, *Drosophila melanogaster* serves as an ideal model organism for the study of IBD pathogenesis [@zeng_drosophila:_2024]. Previous studies have employed the chemical use of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) by acting as the initiating agent in intestinal barrier disruption. DSS exposure elevates ROS, lowers antioxidant defense, and triggers inflammatory cytokine expression, thereby mimicking the pathophysiological features of IBD. Moreover, a Smurf assay can be used to assess intestinal barrier damage by detecting blue dye leakage outside of the gut [@keshav_dextran_2022].

### Clinical Relevance

The implications of this research are multifold, as conventional IBD treatments come with significant side effects, risk of relapse, and non-responsiveness, all failing to address the root cause of collateral damage [@mishra_inflammatory_2020]. As such, there is an urgency for cheaper, safer, and accessible food-based alternatives that patients can integrate into their daily diets. 

### Research Objectives

The purpose of the following study aims to assess how fermentation-enhanced antioxidants from black garlic can attenuate ROS-mediated intestinal damage *in vivo*. Using flightless wild type *Drosophila melanogaster* to model IBD, it is hypothesized that increasing administration of black garlic exhibits a decrease in IBD-like symptoms—gut permeability and lipid peroxidation—in a dose dependent manner due to black garlic’s antioxidant properties. Additionally, lactic acid bacteria fermentation with commercially sourced *Lactobacillus plantarum* confers an additive effect by increasing the availability of metabolites such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and fermentation-derived compounds that contribute to ROS scavenging activity.

## Methods

### Creation of Nonfermented Black Garlic Extract

Organic garlic (*Allium sativum*) was sourced from Unity Farms (Denver, North Carolina). Bulbs were aged in a Tiger® electric rice cooker for 14 days to induce a Maillard reaction and produce black garlic. To perform extraction, 200 g of whole, peeled black garlic bulbs were combined with 2000 mL of 70% ethanol solvent (1:10 w/v) subjected to maceration. During maceration, the components were mildly agitated on a magnetic stirrer for 6 hrs, followed by a period of static diffusion without agitation for 18 hrs. The procedure was repeated using the fresh solvent of identical quality and the resulting macerates were combined and evaporated in a rotary evaporator (40ºC, 42 rpm) until yielding a viscous, molasses-like extract. The black garlic extract (BGE) was reconstituted with deionized water at defined concentrations for administration to *Drosophila melanogaster*.

### Culturing of *Lactobacillus plantarum*

A capsule of commercial *Lactobacillus plantarum* (Vitamatic®, 2.0 × 1010 CFU) was suspended in 10 mL of water. A tenfold serial dilution was performed by transferring 1 mL of the original stock solution into 9 mL of water, which was repeated for 4 successive dilutions. Aliquots of 100 µL from the 10-3 and 10-4 dilutions were spread-plated onto MRS agar plates and incubated at 37ºC. 
To prepare a growth medium, 22.06 g of MRS powder was dissolved in 400 mL of deionized water to obtain MRS broth. The medium was then sterilized by autoclaving with a dry cycle. After incubation, a single isolated colony from the first set of plates was looped into 5 mL of sterile MRS broth and incubated at 37ºC in a shaking incubator until turbidity was observed. A second tenfold serial dilution was performed using the same procedure as above, except carried through five successive dilutions. Aliquots were plated on the 10-4 and 10-5	and incubated in the same conditions. Visible colonies were obtained and were subsequently used for the inoculation of black garlic during the fermentation process.

### Creation of Fermented Black Garlic Extract

To inoculate a known cell density (CFU) into the BGE, two colonies from the second-dilution plates were looped into 20 mL of MRS broth and incubated in a shaking incubation. Optical density at 600 nm ($OD_{600}$) were obtained at regular intervals using a spectrophotometer. Once the culture reached an optical density at 600 nm ($OD_{600}$) of approximately 1, a tenfold serial dilution of the MRS culture was performed following the same procedure described above in order to count cell density post-inoculation. Following this, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was washed by resuspension in saline, followed by a second centrifugation. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in an equivalent volume of BGE. To obtain fermented black garlic extract (F-BGE), the contents were vortexed and then anaerobically fermented at 37ºC for 48 hours.

### Assessment of Antioxidant Activity and Free Radical Scavenging (DPPH Assay)

A standard DPPH assay was conducted using increasing final concentrations of BGE and F-BGE, corresponding to the concentrations modeled for *Drosophila*. In each cuvette, 2800 µL of a 0.1 mM DPPH solution was mixed with varying volumes of anhydrous ethanol and extract stock solution (50 mg/mL). After a 30-min dark incubation period, absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. The same procedure was applied to commercially sourced garlic that underwent the same maceration procedure for comparative analysis with BGE. DPPH % radical scavenging was calculated using the following equation:

$\text{DPPH \% Radical Scavenging} = \frac{A_{\text{control}} - A_{\text{sample}}}{A_{\text{control}}} \times 100$

where $A_{control}$ is the absorbance of DPPH solution only, and $A_{sample}$ is the absorbance of DPPH solution and extract. 

### Stock and Maintenance

Due to the large number of flies needed for this study, a large population of flightless Drosophila were obtained from The Silkworm Shop (San Diego, CA). To obtain females 3-5 days post-eclosion, groups of flies were anesthetized via CO2 exposure (~30 s) and chilled on a flat surface to facilitate handling. Flies were sexed based on morphological characteristics: females were identified by their larger abdominal size and presence of a meconium, while males were identified by a smaller abdominal size and the presence of sex combs on the forelegs. Leading up to experimentation, the flies were kept and maintained in vials containing instant *Drosophila* mix and water in a 1:1 ratio. The initial stock population was bred through routine husbandry practices, including periodic transfer (“flipping”) of adults onto fresh food vials to prevent overcrowding, promote pupal development, and the eclosion of new adults.

### Introduction of IBD in *Drosophila melanogaster*

In this study, intestinal damage was induced in *Drosophila* by administering dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) for 3 days, a protocol commonly applied. Given that DSS must be introduced through their diet, the flies were removed from their standard fly medium diet and transferred to a feeding setup involving filter paper saturated with a sucrose-DSS solution. This setup avoided the confounding effects of the stock diet as well and ensured constant exposure to DSS. 

### Creation of Experimental Groups

Virgin females were placed in vials containing filter paper saturated with 300 µL of 5% sucrose (w/v) and 7% DSS (w/v). Filter papers were replaced every 24 h for the duration of the treatment. Both extracts in the treatment groups were introduced in a 1:1 ratio with the sucrose-DSS solution, with the final in-vial extract concentrations being 5, 10, 25, and 50 mg/mL. 
For the BGE treatment groups, the groups were set up as followed (3 replicates):

    5% sucrose (Control)
	5% sucrose + 7% DSS 
	5% sucrose + 7% DSS + 5 mg/mL BGE 
    5% sucrose + 7% DSS + 10 mg/mL BGE
    5% sucrose + 7% DSS + 25 mg/mL BGE
    5% sucrose + 7% DSS + 50 mg/mL BGE

These groups were then repeated for the F-BGE treatment groups.

### Smurf Assay

Intestinal permeability was conducted through a standard Smurf assay, where filter paper vials containing the sucrose-DSS solution also contained FD&C Blue No. 1 at 0.1% (w/v). Flies were allowed to feed on the dyed solution for 3 days until examination. Filter papers were replaced every 24 hrs for the duration of the treatment. Smurf-positive flies were identified as those that exhibited leakage of blue dye outside the gut and into the surrounding hemocoel. Because each fly was treated as an independent biological replicate, only one set of vials per treatment group was required. 
Following treatment, flies in each treatment group (n = 20) were anesthetized, positioned ventral side-up, and imaged using a stereomicroscope (Leica EZ4). To immobilize the flies, a small drop of clear nail polish was applied to maintain a belly-up orientation. Images were captured using the integrated digital camera under standard lighting conditions to ensure consistency across treatment groups.  
To quantify the extent of dye propagation, images of individual flies were analyzed using ImageJ software. For each fly, the total abdominal region was manually outlined using the freehand tool to obtain the overall abdominal area. The images were converted to HSB stack to visualize them in grayscale, and the blue-stained region was manually selected to calculate the area of dye distribution. Intestinal permeability was expressed as the percentage of blue area relative to total abdominal area. The mean percentage of blue area was calculated for each treatment group and used for analysis, with dead flies excluded. Representative images were captured using a stereomicroscope to visually confirm intestinal permeability.

According to the graph, there is a clear positive dose-dependent trend. As hypothesized, administration of DSS resulted in a dramatic increase in gut permeability (47.99% ± 2.36%) compared to untreated, negative control flies (21.90% ± 2.08%), validating the use of DSS to induce intestinal epithelial damage. 

Both BGE and F-BGE demonstrated significant dose-dependent reductions in DSS-induced permeability. For BGE, mean % blue area decreased from 7.99% ± 2.36% (DSS) to 41.12% ± 2.15% (5 mg/mL), 35.74% ± 2.08% (10 mg/mL), 32.31% ± 1.98% (25 mg/mL), and 27.54% ± 2.01% (50 mg/mL). Similarly, F-BGE reduced permeability, with mean % blue area values of 40.28% ± 2.34% (5 mg/mL), 34.51% ± 2.11% (10 mg/mL), 28.76% ± 1.95% (25 mg/mL), and 25.89% ± 2.07% (50 mg/mL). All concentrations ≥ 10 mg/mL demonstrated significant reductions in permeability.

## Discussion

Current treatments for IBD that act as anti-inflammatory agents, such as 5-aminosalicylates (5-ASA), corticosteroids, thiopurines, anti-TNFα antibodies primarily induce remission rather than provide a definitive cure (Mishra, 2020). Given that inflammation promotes an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to lipid peroxidation and generation of malondialdehyde (MDA), natural antioxidant-based interventions represent a relevant strategy for attenuating intestinal epithelial damage in IBD. 

The findings of the existing study demonstrate that  black garlic exhibits antioxidant activity *in vitro*, as confirmed by DPPH radical scavenging, and reduces intestinal permeability *in vivo* in *Drosophila*. Together, these findings suggest that the antioxidant properties of black garlic may contribute to the attenuation of inflammatory stress on the intestinal epithelial barrier. 

###  Effect of Lactic Acid Bacteria Fermentation on Antioxidant Outcome 

Based on the results of the DPPH radical scavenging assay, lactic acid bacteria fermentation did not enhance the overall antioxidant capacity of black garlic extract under the conditions tested (@Figure_3). Such findings contrast with existing literature, which reported increases in total polyphenol content from ~2.30 mg/mL to 2.69 ± 0.01 mg/mL following *L. rhamnosus* fermentation, while total flavonoids increased from ~1.25 mg/mL to 1.79 ± 0.08 mg/mL in other groups (Ma, 2021). Phenolic and flavonoid compounds are recognized as contributors to antioxidant capacity. However, they do not directly translate to DPPH scavenging efficacy. Further experimentation is required to elucidate whether fermentation altered bioactive compounds such as sulfur-containing derivatives and Maillard reaction products (e.g., melanoidins), both of which are known to contribute to the antioxidant capacity of black garlic (Ro, 2022).   

### *In vivo* Attenuation of Intestinal Permeability

Despite exhibiting reduced DPPH radical scavenging compared to BGE, F-BGE demonstrated statically equivalent mean % blue area at all concentrations tested (@Figure_7). This discrepancy between *in vitro* antioxidant capacity and *in vivo* efficacy may suggest ROS-independent protective mechanisms. For example, probiotic-derived metabolites such as organic acids and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) may enhance mucus layer integrity and maintain the integrity of the gut barrier by stimulating various signaling pathways (Zeng, 2024). These fermentation-derived compounds likely account for F-BGE’s preserved therapeutic activity despite reduced antioxidant capacity. 

### Limitations

Observed differences in intestinal permeability could either be attributed to genuine protective efficacy or extract intake. Thus, a key limitation of this study is the absence of a quantitative feeding assay (e.g., capillary feeding or CAFE assay) to confirm an equal extract consumption across treatment groups. Black garlic is nontoxic to *Drosophila* at concentrations up to 4 mg/mL (Toledano Medina, 2019), but the maximum concentration administered in this experiment (50 mg/mL) exceeds this range and may influence feeding behavior through taste aversion or deterrence. Flies fed on dyed solutions at all concentrations, evidenced by blue coloration in the gut, but precise consumption was not quantified. Notably, no acute toxicity was observed at any concentration, confirming that the extracts are well-tolerated even at doses exceeding previously accepted thresholds. 

### Future Directions

Future work should employ a biochemical quantification of malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in whole-body fly homogenates. This could be achieved through a thiobarbituric acid (TBA)-based assay, in which MDA reacts with TBA to form a pink chromogenic adduct that is measurable spectrophotometrically. A reduction in absorbance would indicate decreased lipid peroxidation. Such measurements would establish whether the observed gut barrier protection directly correlates with an inhibition of ROS-induced lipid peroxidation to the membrane, or if there are alternative protective mechanisms. Similar DSS-induced *Drosophila* IBD studies have measured MDA following antioxidant treatment to confirm reductions in oxidative damage to the intestinal epithelial membrane.

As such, biochemical markers such as MDA would effectively link lipid peroxidation to improvements in intestinal integrity. 

## Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that black garlic extract exhibits dose-dependent protective effects against DSS-induced intestinal barrier damage in *Drosophila melanogaster* due to its ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species and mitigate oxidative stress. Black garlic emerges as a promising candidate for accessible food-based treatments for attenuating gut permeability in IBD. Derived from a widely available food source and producible at home (rice cooker aging), this research demonstrates how natural compounds can be utilized as a therapeutic agent for chronic inflammatory conditions through cost-effective and patient-accessible interventions.
