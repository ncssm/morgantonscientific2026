title: "Exercise-based Interventions in Parkinson's Disease: A Pilot Study"
abstract: |
  Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurological disorder that affects 60% of the United States population; the severity of this disease continues to grow, which disrupts motor capabilities and affects quality of life. PD has several therapies, including neurosurgery, pharmacological interventions, and traditional physical therapy, though some may not be available for individuals as the disease progresses. Therefore, there is an increasing need for novel interventions to treat motor dysfunction. This study aimed to utilize a novel non-motor intervention to improve gait function. Participants completed three sessions, spaced 24 and 72 hours apart. During each session, participants completed an obstacle course as many times as possible within three minutes, aiming to minimize errors. Gait initiation and steady-state gait were evaluated before and after the first and last sessions. There was a significant reduction in errors and reaction time (*p*<0.05). Four separate ANOVAs were conducted to show the effects of the model on step length and variability between initial steps and steady state. A significant improvement was observed in the length of the first step of gait initiation. No significant changes were detected in other gait parameters. This study demonstrated that the novel intervention is feasible and can improve reaction time, reduce errors, and enhance the initial step length during gait initiation. Although no other significant gait changes occurred, the results suggest potential benefits. Future research with a longer duration and larger sample size is warranted to explore further this intervention's efficacy and potential learning effects.

## Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a complex, progressive neurological disorder that James Parkinson first described in 1817. Symptoms of PD are characterized by motor and nonmotor symptoms. Common motor symptoms include Akinesia, the inability to initiate voluntary movements; Bradykinesia, the slowness of movement and tremors, particularly in the head and hands; freezing of gait (FoG); rigidity, reduced stride length, and diminished arm swinging [@armstrong_diagnosis_2020]. Nonmotor symptoms include depression, trouble sleeping, anxiety, hallucinations, and apathy [@bonnet_amplifying_2013]. These symptoms can vary in presentation and severity among individuals with PD, highlighting the heterogeneous nature of the disease. 

To slow the progression of the disease, pharmacological methods have been introduced, like Levodopa, which increases dopamine levels [@lang_emotion_1998]. Additionally, the combination of pharmacological and physical activity (PA) has proven beneficial (Petzinger et al., 2013). However, there have been cases where individuals develop Levodopa resistance, which may result in worsening motor deficits [@lang_emotion_1998]. Therefore, it is pertinent to explore more nonpharmacological interventions [@nonnekes_unmasking_2016]. 

Additionally, neurophysical treatments have demonstrated through research that combining physical and cognitive interventions, such as brain stimulation and mental imaging, induces neuroplasticity. These treatments have displayed effective results in decreasing the severity of the motor and nonmotor symptoms and inducing neuroplasticity—the ability of the brain to learn and adapt based on an injury [@debu_managing_2018].

This pilot study focuses on investigating nonpharmacological interventions for PD with an emphasis on physical activity-based approaches. The interventions include complex walking, dual-tasking, balance, and strength training. Previous research has shown that having a more multidisciplinary approach leads to a more comprehensive and reassuring approach to Parkinson's disease treatment [@debu_managing_2018]. This means using both pharmaceutical treatments and physical interventions to further learning benefits. Furthermore, implementing motor imagery into a physical activity protocol has positively affected PD. By increasing cognitive awareness in the dorsal visual pathway and deep locomotion activities, motor imagery was a promising approach to PD treatment [@scarpina_effect_2019]. 

The primary aim of this study was to assess the improvement or deterioration that motor imagery showed on a complex walking task. This study hypothesized that combining motor imagery and a complex walking task would induce neuroplasticity. Neuroplasticity increases learning in the brain, which can reduce the severity and progression of motor deficits. Complex walking tasks, such as obstacle courses, may provide an efficient method to evaluate the learning effects.  These effects measure responsiveness and explore the reliability of this treatment, and they aim to provide a comprehensive approach to PD treatment. 

## Literature Review

### Anatomy of the Brain

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a disorder characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, which controls motor and cognitive behaviors [@lang_emotion_1998].  The body needs dopamine for neurons to communicate and function properly. Dopaminergic neurons are the neurons that produce dopamine. The basal ganglia are also affected, which control learning and selection in motor function (Skinner et al., 2020). Through the progression of PD, a decrease in dopamine production and loss of functionality over time are observed. The reduced dopamine levels associated with PD negatively affect motor and nonmotor function [@lang_emotion_1998].

The motor cortex (M1) is located around the outside of the brain above the ears. This region of the brain controls the movements in the body. Due to the loss of dopamine in the basal ganglia, the communication between it, the M1, and the thalamus is lost. Therefore, the motor functions deteriorate as the disease progresses in severity.  Parkinson's is mainly an idiopathic disorder, meaning the origin of the disease is unknown [@lang_emotion_1998]. About 10-15% of PD cases are caused by genetics, and previous research has shown that there are dozens of genetic mutations linked to the disease. Because the definitive cause of PD has yet to be discovered, searching for effective treatments and interventions, especially for motor symptoms, remains crucial.

### Motor Imagery

Motor imagery (MI) and action observation (AO) have been shown to affect individuals with severe PD positively. In the study written by Mezzarobba et al. in 2024, MI and AO are studied for their effects on PD. As stated in the study, action observation is primarily driven by visual stimuli, such as observing others' behavior, and is associated with activation in occipital regions. On the other hand, Motor imaging relies on internally generated stimuli involving the reactivation of motor representations stored in memory [@mezzarobba_action_2024].

MI and AO activate the premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, inferior frontal gyrus, intraparietal area, primary motor cortex, superior parietal lobule, and inferior parietal lobule. This activation is a stepping stone to physically completing a specific task because it allows the brain to activate and learn without physical action. Additionally, this activation aids with gait, FoG, mobility, and balance. According to previous literature, AO and MI are efficient and accessible forms of inducing motor learning without physical movements [@mezzarobba_action_2024].

The review by @mezzarobba_action_2024 found that AO and MI can induce neuroplasticity and improve Berg balance scores, Tinetti scale scores, and gait speed. The Berg test is a balance test, and the Tinetti scale is a balance and gait scale. Moreover, there was an improvement in mean TUG score, push and release tests, 360-degree tests, and ADLs. These tests assess mobility and postural instability. Similarly, in the upper body, the nine-hole peg test supported that hand dexterity also increased. However, other studies in the review tested AO and MI together with other physical-based interventions. These interventions showed preliminary improvements in dexterity and reaction time. Post-tests of participants showed brain reorganization of motor controls and memory. This led to long-term improvements in dual-task, mobility, and balance [@mezzarobba_action_2024].

### Neuroplasticity

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a surgical intervention that aims to combat the deterioration of motor neurons [@balestrino_parkinson_2020; @leemhuis2021deep]. DBS supplies electrical impulses to brain areas, including the subthalamic nucleus, the thalamus, or the globus pallidus internus. The subthalamic nucleus is vital to the basal ganglia, contributing to motor functions and cognitive abilities. Similarly, the globus pallidus internus regulates voluntary movements. On the other hand, the thalamus receives sensory messages related to motor functions. Targeting these brain regions can ease motor symptoms and alleviate medication reliance [@brooks_deep_2021].

Nonsurgical interventions such as physical activity have also been shown to induce neuroplasticity [@petzinger_exercise-enhanced_2013]. An 8-week aerobic training program combined with dopamine receptors induced motor engagement and learning [@petzinger_exercise-enhanced_2013]. Promoting motor engagement and learning is known to decrease the severity of motor deficits [@petzinger_exercise-enhanced_2013]. Furthermore, 1-hour sessions of strength and balance training performed 15 times/week for four weeks were shown to decrease motor symptoms.  Biking, dancing, and occupational and physical therapy have also been identified as exercise-based modalities for inducing neuroplasticity and reducing motor deficits [@johansson_exercise-induced_2020].

Furthermore, in a study by Walton et al., 65 randomized trials focusing on cognitive training reported overall improvements in neurophysiological performance. These improvements include gait improvements, such as step length and variability, freezing of gait, and brain state, such as the amount of white vs gray matter and cortical thickness. For the most part, Walton et al. described that most positive effects occur when training is at least 30-minute sessions for a maximum of three days a week. Therefore, cognitive training effectively induces neuroplasticity and improves functions [@walton_cognitive_2018].

### Dual Tasking/Complex Walking

Dual-task interventions have positively impacted mobility, activities of daily living (ADLs), and gait parameters such as step length and variability. Complex walking tasks (CWTs) require individuals to adapt to changing tasks and environmental stimuli beyond the physiological resources needed for regular, less complicated walking [@shumwaycook_ageassociated_2007]. Some examples of CWTs include treadmill walking, virtual reality (VR), and navigating obstacle courses. CWTs have improved motor performance in at least six weeks [@pelosin_multimodal_2020]. Using VR to increase the difficulty of treadmill walking, Pelosin et al. noted acute decreases in fall rate and gait variability (2020). To elaborate, in a study conducted by @pelosin_multimodal_2020, treadmill training combined with virtual reality was used to simulate complex walking. Participants trained three times a week for six weeks. This led to improved motor performance and an acute decrease in fall rate and step variability.

### Balance Training

Balance training has also improved motor functions in persons with PD [@conradsson_effects_2015]. Conradsson et al. used the HiBalance program to test the effects of balance interventions on PD. Participants trained for three 60-minute sessions per week for ten weeks.  Pre-test to post-test results showed statistically insignificant data in all categories outside of dual tasking.  Therefore, it is supported that balance training improves motor functions to a slight extent (2015). Additionally, in a study by Tamine Teixeira da Costa Capato et al., 150 PD patients were randomly distributed among three groups. The first group received motor training focused on balance with rhythmic auditory cues. The second group received the same training as the first, but without the cues, and the control group only received exercises in general orientation. Each group underwent experimentation for ten sessions, twice a week for 45 minutes. The outcome of this study was that including rhythmical auditory cues in the motor program can help improve balance in PD patients.

### Equipment

A 16m Protokinetics electronic walkway (gait mat) was used independently from the obstacle course. The gait mat was used in the pretest and post-test to measure the gait variability in individuals before and after the intervention. However, RoxPro lights guided the participants through the obstacle course. These were randomized lights that directed the individual toward one of three loops in the course and recorded reaction time data. Two appeared red, while one appeared green, which indicated which path the participant would follow. Reaction time was recorded based on three Rox lights at the halfway point of each loop. This measured the time for the participant to reach the halfway point. The participant started behind two infrared Brower sensors. Once the participant crosses, the sensors beep, indicating the start of the measurements for that round. Once the participant passed the Rox on the loop, a 10 to 15-second delay started before the next light set was displayed. Brower infrared sensors were used to time individuals. Time was calculated from when the participant crossed the sensor to when the participant returned. The iPad stopwatch application was used to time the trials manually as a backup measure.

To construct the obstacle course, a variety of low-intensity obstacles were utilized, including a wobble board, small discs and cones, a small staircase, 12-18 inch hurdles, inflatable balance pads (Dyna Discs), foam balance surfaces (Airex Pads), and doorways. These obstacles were distributed into a three-loop course curated to aid in CAI and PD. 

### Loop A

Loop A was a target loop where participants targeted the obstacles. The loop was 12.95m with a straightaway and a curve. The straightaway consisted of two wobble boards: a square that was 48 cm long and 8 cm high, and a circle with a diameter of 48 cm and also 8 cm high. In Loop A, participants encountered two wobble boards while walking through the straightaway and four 32cm Dyna Discs; the first three were more deflated at 8cm, while the last one was inflated to 16cm to add difficulty while walking through the loop's curve. This design targeted all basic ankle movements (@Figure_1).

### Statistics

A Fit Model ANOVA was constructed to examine the data between group, loop, and session. The analysis of variance was found with an effect test, resulting in loop and session being the main effects. Aside from this, loop-by-session data was affected to a slightly lower extent. Post Hoc tests, Tukey's honestly significant differences (HSD), were run to calculate the mean time to completion (TTC) and the significant differences of loops A, B, and C. Four separate ANOVA analyses were conducted to examine the effects of the model on step length variability (CV) across different gait phases: 1st step, 2nd step, 3rd step, and steady state.

### Protocol

The intervention used was a complex walking course. Before testing, participants underwent a pretest on the 16m protokinetic electronic pathway. This pretest measured participants' step length and variability in gait initiation and steady-state gait. Next, all participants had a five to ten-minute introduction to the study's objectives, procedures, and safety guidelines and completed the informed consent. Participants then underwent three obstacle course sessions. The reaction and amount of time on loops were measured using the RoxPro and Brower systems. At the end and beginning of each loop, the participant remained behind the Brower sensors. These sensors measured split times and total times for the entire obstacle course. However, the Rox system measured reaction time and directed participants. Sessions were initiated once the first green light was presented. Once initiated, the participant followed the green light to complete as many laps as possible in three minutes. However, only the first nine laps were considered for this study. The first session included a three-minute walk, a ten-minute rest, and a final three-minute period. 

Twenty-four hours later, the experimental group completed the motor imagery session while the control group walked the obstacle course again. Participants were randomly assigned to an experimental and a control group. The six participants in the experimental group engaged in motor imagery by watching a three-minute video of another person partaking in the obstacle course and imagining themselves trying to complete the task done in the second session of the intervention. The remaining six in the control group performed the obstacle course without watching any video. The tests and complex walking tasks were scheduled and timed the same way as the participants who did not use the motor imagery intervention.

Three days after the initial session, the third session occurred. This session included a three-minute walk in which the participant attempted to complete as many rounds as possible. Lastly, to measure the outcomes of this testing, the number and duration of loops, errors, reaction time, and time spent on each loop were noted. Also noted during the sessions were feedback, strategies, concerns, and observed challenges from the participants. Lastly, the gait outcomes of complex walking and motor imagery were measured using a post-test on the 16m Protokinetics electronic pathway. Like the pre-test, this test measured step length and variability in both gait initiation and steady-state gait. With all protocols considered, no unsafe measures were taken, and the risks were deemed ethical. 

## Results

### Demographics

Twelve total participants were tested. Age was the primary inclusion factor for this pilot study, with individuals being 15+ years old. Individuals with a neurological disease, a reported history of CAI, or people with a recent history of disability or injury were excluded. Twelve participants were split evenly into motor imagery and obstacle course-only groups. Both groups held similar averages in height, weight, and leg length (@Table_1). Sexes were equally distributed in both groups. Age had a significant difference between the groups ( *p*=0.049). The MI group age was 15.8±0.8 years, while the OC Group age was 16.7±0.5 years.

## Discussions

### Overview

In this pilot study, the learning effects of a week-long intervention with an emphasis on motor and cognitively challenging walking tasks in 12 participants were studied. The study's results provide insights into the feasibility and effectiveness of these interventions in enhancing individuals' motor performance and cognitive engagement. The findings of this trial revealed that MI alone does not have significant effects on loop, session, and time. However, when compared, MI integrated with CWTs demonstrates greater learning effects. The significant changes in data include the time of the first step in gait initiation, errors, and reaction time. These findings support the potential utility of combined MI and physical activity interventions for improving motor and cognitive outcomes. 

### Effects of Intervention

Based on the above data, errors decreased from the first to the last session during the obstacle course. This error reduction suggests that participants adapted to the tasks and improved their motor skills over the intervention period. The session and loop are the factors that determine the number of errors in the data presented. This is indicated by the ANOVA results, (F(2,18)=5.9495, p<0.0035) and (F(2,18)=6.4525, p<0.0114). This finding aligns with previous research indicating that task complexity and repetition play crucial roles in motor learning and skill acquisition [@pelosin_multimodal_2020]. 

Secondly, reaction time improved with the intervention. The main effect of reaction time was the loop completed with the equation (F(35,18)=3.3669, p<0.0156). This improvement suggests that the CWTs effectively enhanced participants' cognitive processing and decision-making abilities, essential for navigating complex environments. The integration of MI further supported this enhancement by engaging the participants' cognitive resources and mental rehearsal of the tasks [@mezzarobba_action_2024]. 

Thirdly, the first step in gait initiation was improved. The main reason for this change was the duration of the intervention. This means that with more time completing the intervention, there will be a higher learning effect observed through the first step of gait initiation. This can be shown by (F(1,20)=5.5039, p<0.0294). 

Between the 10-minute pre- and 72-hour post-sessions, there was no significant improvement in time to complete each loop of the obstacle course, demonstrating no immediate learning effect. Despite the results, the use of obstacle courses in future studies has not been invalidated. Alternatively, it provides researchers with valuable insights into enhancing their study design, protocol, and methods to improve the possibility of observing learning effects for other complex walking task research in the future. This study's results demonstrate no significant effects on step length variability across any gait phases through this model.

### Limitations and Future Research

This study has limitations. First, many technological errors occurred within the BROWER system and manual timing. This led to incomplete and/or invalid data in some cases. Secondly, the original plan for the experiment had to be adapted due to a lack of time and usable materials. This caused the original, extended experiment to decrease drastically throughout this research. This may be a reason for the insignificance of the data collected, which led to an unsupported hypothesis. Thirdly, the sample size was only twelve people, with little diversity, and needed to be generalized to the public. These constraints and limitations can be mended in future research and the legitimate study following this pilot study.

In the future, this study would ideally have an increased sample size, variables to test, more sessions, more controlled external factors, and a higher technological class. An increased sample size would allow for more conclusive and general data that can be used to help a more significant amount of the public. Similarly, if more variables were to be tested during this intervention, then more diverse learning effects may appear after the intervention. Also, using more variables, this research will be more generalized and helpful to a larger population. Additionally, more sessions of this intervention will possibly demonstrate greater learning effects over time. The literature review indicated that studies lasting multiple weeks with three sessions a week had the most promising results. An increase in sessions will also increase the longevity of the results. This will lead to fewer acute effects and an improved state of living for those with PD. Based on uncontrolled external factors, the data collected may be skewed. These can include watching a participant complete the course, personal injuries, and demonstrating the course to participants.

Lastly, with newer and more dependable technology, the data pulled from participants will be more accurate and less likely to pose invalid information.

## Conclusion

To conclude, evidence is provided that motor imagery integrated into complex walking tasks can induce neuroplasticity and increase learning effects. Improvements in the step length and errors throughout the study are shown. The second and third steps proved statistically significant in an increase after the interventions. The errors decreased significantly based on insults in the third session. These enhancements suggest that combining MI with CWTs not only optimizes recovery but also improves the effectiveness of treatment for individuals with PD. This enables a more enjoyable and healthy lifestyle for those with PD. While complex walking has been shown to improve abilities on its own, the combination of CWTs and MI may optimize the recovery and effectiveness of treatment. 

A more significant learning effect may be observed if CWTs are integrated with motor imagery. Our research has refuted this hypothesis, as there is insufficient evidence of significant learning effects when using the obstacle course and motor imagery. Certain aspects, such as gait initiation, were improved and could be validated in future research. Future research should continue to explore these methods to validate their benefits further. The findings of this study should be expanded by conducting more extensive, longer studies to confirm the long-term benefits of combining MI and CWTs for PD. Future research should include at least 12 participants and three sessions repeated. Motor imagery should be tested with other physical-based interventions to determine if there are any significant learning effects.
